SHADOWVEIL Lore:
An open-world, classless, systems driven singleplayer RPG.
Logistics: 1 Block = 1 Cubic Meter.
Height Limit: 100 Blocks High.
Map Size: 1,149,184 blocks², 4,489 chunks (16 blocks × 16 blocks = 1 chunk)
World Dimensions: 1,072 × 1,072 blocks (67 × 67 chunks)
Gameworld = Ashenwrath (The Continent's Name)
RPGgame = SHADOWVEIL (the name of the playable area)
The playable build remains a systems/mechanics devmap. The geography below is the production-world design.
The game starts on Solday, May 5th, 500.

Skills:

	Combat
		- Attack - Land real melee hits. Better XP from chained hits, punishing missed enemy swings, counterattacks after real blocks/parries/dodges, and weapon specials that actually connect. Attack improves tempo/handling, not hit chance or damage.
		- Strength - Do real physical force: land melee damage, use heavy committed attacks, cause real staggers, land high-impact weapon specials, or lift/push/reposition meaningful objects.
		- Defense - Take real hostile HP damage (always gives qualified Defense XP), and/or successfully perform real weapon guards, shield blocks, active dodges, and timed parries. No passive evasion XP.
		- Health - Do meaningful exertion during travel/work, endure real injury in danger, restore genuinely missing Health through valid recovery, survive a real hostile encounter after injury, or survive a high-threat encounter while seriously depleted.
		- Archery - Physically hit targets with thrown darts/knives/tomahawks/javelins or crossbow projectiles. More XP for moving/evading targets and real weak-point/opening hits.
		- Magic - Cast spells that actually affect combat or the world: useful enhancements, real perception changes, meaningful traversal, damaging spell hits, burn buildings and foliage down or real social/identity effects. Books are Sun (Elemental), Moon (Dark) and Fog (Necrotic)
		- Faith - Bury genuinely acquired eligible bones, get real benefit from active stat prayers, protect against real incoming attacks with overhead prayers, sustain effective prayer use through a demanding fight, or complete supported spiritual purification. Idle prayer drain gives no XP.

	Gathering
		- Woodcutting - Clear usable vegetation with a machete, split real logs into firewood, fell smaller/larger trees, or complete a demanding controlled tree fell. Requires appropriate axe/hatchet/machete work.
		- Mining - Excavate clay, cut quarry stone, extract coal, mine ore from metal veins, or work difficult gem-bearing deposits with a pickaxe.
		- Fishing - Make real catches using the correct method: small net, rod, harpoon, lobster pot, big net, etc. XP follows the actual catch and effort, not placing gear.
		- Harvesting - Prepare unready soil, plant real seeds/saplings, water plants that actually need it, prune/care for plants with a real maintenance need, or harvest mature crops/herbs/yields.

	Production
		- Cooking - Successfully cook real food or meals at a fire/stove. XP comes from the recipe/food prepared, not the station. Burnt/failed attempts give no successful-cook XP.
		- Crafting - Make real finished objects: cut/stitch leather or cloth, shape clay/glass, cut gems, assemble wearables/furnishings, or finish high-difficulty molded jewelry/symbol crafts.
		- Smithing - Smelt real bars, repair damaged metal equipment, forge functional metal tools, forge weapons/armor components, or complete an eligible within-tier refinement at an anvil with a hammer.
		- Construction - Build/install real furnishings, repair genuinely damaged fixtures, build valid structural segments, install functioning workstations, or finish multi-stage estate construction projects in Building Mode. No XP for buying, previewing, moving, or demolishing.

	Utility
		- Agility - Do real traversal: vault low obstacles, cross precarious surfaces, make meaningful gap jumps, sprint, complete demanding vertical climbs, or escape an actual pursuit using connected traversal. No XP for idle jumps, walking or teleports.
		- Firemaking - Ignite a new supported campfire with tinderbox and logs, relight a legitimately extinguished fire, refuel a fire that genuinely lacks fuel, prepare a useful work/cooking fire, or complete a real charcoal-fuel conversion.
		- Thieving - Pickpocket a real carried item, steal from an owned open container, pick an eligible secured door, open an eligible secured chest/container, or extract property from actively guarded premises. No XP for looking, searching, or reopening the same lock.

	Skill-action rule: Ordinary skill XP requires performing the skill’s real world action and getting a committed result. No action XP for menus, purchases, idle time, animations without results, fake targets, or repeated state-reset farming. Authored one-time main-quest milestone rewards are the separate exception described under Quests. All skills cap at 100; level 100 needs 6,000,000 cumulative XP. Health starts at level 10 with 43,446 XP; every other skill starts at level 1 with 0 XP.

LORE / WORLD:
	Notes: Players first spawn in Loxford Castle upon creating their profile.
	Seven settled powers: Loxford, Azharad, Severia, Valgard, Gorrick, Harlette and Camelot. Wilderness lies between them; ocean surrounds the playable square. Rivers and lakes drain to the sea, woods support Woodcutting, and ore, farms and fisheries sustain the settlements.
	Every NPC in the game has to have a designated home and bed besides bounty hunters. e.g. bandits live in camps in the wilderness, woodcutters might live in large motel like structures in towns. Each kingdom should have at least 2-3 of blue-collar essential worker NPC types. Be realistic. Names should be mononyms inspired by GRRM and Tolkien.

	2. Azharad Sultanate
		Size: Urban and oasis core 169 chunks, 43,264 blocks²; quarry and caravan approaches beyond.
		Visual Tint: warm, golden, hazy, sun-bleached, nostalgic Orange/amber, high-contrast sepia.
		Culture: Urban, clanish, textual, commercial, and honor-coded. Lineage and law sit in tension; hospitality and reputation are social currency; prestige flows through learning, argument, poetry, and patronage. Cosmopolitan in trade and scholarship, moralistic in public norms.

		Map Location: Southeast; core [49–61,6–18].
		Ruler: Sultan Fahad Al-Saladin

Its households built wealth by keeping contracts and sheltering travellers, but Fahad's court increasingly decides which family version of a promise counts as law. Workshops need open roads more than they need another display of dynastic honour.

Gorrick buys supplies for frontier expeditions; Camelot's escorts offer an alternative to imperial patrol fees. The Sultan opposes any compact that lets a Sun court inspect Moon-kept household books without consent.

	3. Severian Empire
		Size: Urban core and east housing 426 chunks, 109,056 blocks²; northern coast, vineyards and approaches beyond.
		Visual Tint: bright, clear, high-key ochre and whitewash with sea-blue accents, sun-drenched (distinct from Azharad's low amber lamplight).

	4. Valgard Kingdom
		Size: Town core 70 chunks, 17,920 blocks²; mining slopes and service roads extend north and west.
		Visual Tint: pale limestone, cold daylight, white banners and the orange glow of working furnaces.
		Culture: Law, public service and military discipline define standing. A knight's oath is entered beside a mason's wage and a miner's injury claim; both are supposed to bind the powerful. Guild families control the skills and credit behind the crown's armies.
		Vibe: a stern Sun capital built on the backs of working people.
		Map Location: Western interior; town [6–15,25–31], Sunkissed Mine [10,34], Marble Mine [6,33].

		History & Conflict: Valgard grew around the western ore road and the principal sanctuary of Jaehaerys. Its crown became the military and legal heart of the Sun kingdoms by protecting roads and standardising sworn contracts. Phillip wants law to outlast personal favour, but his own armouries depend on guild advances and exhausted miners. The White Knights guard roads and public courts; the Temple Knights protect sanctuaries, records and relics. Their oaths overlap, while their loyalties do not always agree.
		Relations: Loxford supplies grain and timber; Azharad buys metal and sells finished craft goods; Severia handles seaborne contracts. Gorrick resists Valgard's claim to inspect every northern shipment. Phillip recognises Arthur's crown but distrusts a court whose personal loyalties can outrank written law. Harlette's produce is welcome; Dracula's household practices are not.

	5. Gorrick
		Size: Town core 25 chunks, 6,400 blocks².
		Visual Tint: wet timber, slate, iron-black workshops and warm monastery windows above a grey lake.
		Culture: Household assemblies choose whom to trust, and a chief is judged by winter stores, fair shares and whether patrols come home. Written contracts coexist uneasily with witnessed promises. Outsiders can earn a place through work; inherited rank alone buys little.
		Vibe: a hard frontier refuge with an arsenal behind its monastery.
		Map Location: West shoulder of Dimwater [22–26,40–44]; town centre [24,42], monastery [23,43].
		Ruler: Chieftain Torkell
		History & Conflict: The settlement was once called Hojimo, a refuge whose keepers recorded travellers lost in the Fog without demanding proof of lineage. The present monastery took up Sun rites but retained the old name rolls and a place for Moon-kept testimony. Torkell united the work camps after outside patrols stopped arriving. Gorrick now sells repaired arms, charcoal and expedition supplies to the very courts it distrusts; closing its gates would starve the town before it humbled them.
		Relations: Valgard provides iron, Severia credit, and Camelot trained escorts. Torkell accepts contracts but rejects an outside garrison. The Hojimo registers make Gorrick important to the Lantern dispute: people the courts never counted still paid for its roads and died defending them.
		Major Locations:
			Chieftain's Hall - Assembly room, common hearth, Torkell's chambers and watch bunks. [24,42]
			Hojimo Monastery - Sun altar, old name rolls, guest cells and a protected witness room. [23,43]
			Gorrick Bank - Secured expedition deposits beside the return road. [24,41]
			Iron Yard - Repair forge, arms store and charcoal exchange. [25,42]

	Quests:
		Structure:
			A quest is a state machine with four parts. Stages are numbered states such as 1, 2, and 3, and the quest knows only its current stage. Objectives are journal entries and minimap markers; they are independent of stages, so several can be active at once. Aliases are named slots that resolve at runtime to an NPC, item, or location; objectives, dialogue, and conditions reference the alias rather than a fixed object, which lets the quest tolerate world-state changes. Triggers are scripts or conditions that advance stages, such as entering a trigger volume, acquiring an item, an actor dying, or a dialogue line ending. The defining property is that the quest records that an event occurred, never how the player caused it. That single constraint accounts for most of the perceived dynamism. Main and side quests use the same underlying quest system. The distinction is their narrative purpose, progression role, teaching responsibilities, and reward structure. Both can use guided or open quest structures, multiple objectives, aliases, triggers, dialogue branches, and the additive memory layer. Main quests are responsible for character development, skill progression, and the central storyline. Side quests are responsible for expanding the world, developing NPCs, creating optional activities, and providing non-XP rewards.

			Guided quests. A guided quest is a sequence of gates, each with one completion event. A hook such as dialogue, a story manager event, or a location trigger starts the quest at stage 1. Each stage carries one objective, one marker, and one gate event. The space between gates is hand-built environment, and the quest does not evaluate behavior within it. Authoring rules: one gate per stage with an unmistakable marker; multiple routes between gates; gate on outcomes such as acquiring the item, never on methods such as killing five enemies.
			Open quests. Open quests use the same engine with different gate semantics. Multiple objectives are active at once and completable in any order. Converging stages let several paths set the same stage. Conditions are state checks such as whether the player has an item or is in a location, rather than step checks. A convergence gate must evaluate the quest's intended completion condition, such as all required objectives being complete or any one of several accepted paths being completed. Dialogue branches may set different flags while converging on the same beat. The shape is an hourglass: broad freedom within a segment, narrowing through a checkpoint into the next segment. The funnel preserves narrative coherence while the player experiences freedom of approach.
			Safety nets. Essential flags on main quest-critical NPCs prevent soft-locks. Alias fallbacks handle missing or dead targets. Late-stage triggers fire on location arrival even if the player never spoke to the quest giver. Re-checkable gates let a missed trigger still fire later. Some quests are locked behind skill checks, such as requiring a certain level in a skill before unlocking the ability to start or trigger them. Skill requirements may be used by either quest type, but they do not change the reward rules: side quests do not award XP, even when they require an existing skill.

			The additive memory layer. The state machine records progression but not context. The memory layer records context for presentation and acknowledgement without altering progression. It has two primitives. Stage entry reasons: the stage-setting call accepts a reason tag such as disguise, sneak, bribe, combat, skipped quest giver, or arrived early; the stage number still drives progression, and the reason is recorded alongside it. Fact ledger: triggers write booleans and counters independent of stages, such as bribed guard, used shout in town, witnessed theft, or alias is dead; stages do not read these to advance, but presentation systems do.
			Memory layer consumers. Conditional objective variants present the same objective ID with different journal text and markers based on facts. Alias state memory records states such as met, hostile, owes favor, witnessed theft, dead, or replaced. Cross-quest fact reading lets barks, guards, schedules, and other quests read facts without depending on the originating quest's stages. Partial and epilogue stages, such as 4 and 5, acknowledge deaths, skips, or witnesses without gating the main funnel. Reconciliation generalizes the late-stage trigger into a pass that runs on load, cell change, alias change, and quest start, backfilling stages for players who advanced ahead of sequence.
			Constraint: facts must not become required method gates, and stages must not branch on every fact; that produces combinatorial growth and quests that look reactive in the editor and fail in play. Facts serve acknowledgement, while the funnel remains the progression mechanism. Tooling is required: a fact graph viewer, unreachable-stage checker, trigger coverage report, and soft-lock checker, because polish degrades immediately when the quest fails.
			Templates in plain terms. Guided: hook is the initiation event; stage 1 objective is travel to a place, gate is a trigger volume, reason is a route tag; stage 2 objective is acquire an alias item, gate is item in inventory, reason is a route tag; stage 3 objective is return to an alias NPC, gate is dialogue ends, reason is a dialogue branch tag; stage 10 is complete with reward; safety includes NPC essential, item quest-critical, alias fallback, and late-stage trigger on arrival; facts are written by triggers.
			Open: hook is the initiation event; stage 1 has objectives A, B, and C active in any order, each method sets a flag and writes facts; stage 2 gate evaluates the configured completion condition, such as all required objectives being completed or an accepted alternative path being completed; the reason records the first or relevant completion event; stage 3 objective is the shared next beat; stages 4 and 5 are optional epilogue variants and can extend up to 9 stages selected by facts; stage 10 is complete; safety includes a skip-ahead trigger that sets an appropriate continuation state on arrival and a reconciliation pass that backfills on load and cell change. Quests must implement stage entry reasons with conditional dialogue, barks, and journal summaries. Stages, gates, and convergence points remain unchanged, but the world acknowledges the route taken.

		Main Quests:
			Structure:
				Main quests are SHADOWVEIL's primary narrative progression. They teach and deepen skills through consequential situations, advance the central story and award an intentional one-time mainline XP reward for the skills genuinely exercised. Main quests teach skills through introduction, instruction, practice and application within the story. Ordinary skill XP still requires real actions. Side quests remain optional and do not award quest-completion XP.
				Main quests use guided stages for dramatic sequences and open stages when evidence, locations or contacts can be pursued in any order. A stage advances because an outcome occurred, not because the player used one prescribed method. Conversation, exploration, stealth, combat, tools and alliances are supported where the situation genuinely permits them. Routes converge on a clear narrative beat, while journal text, dialogue, schedules, access and later scenes acknowledge the route actually taken.
				Quest-critical people and evidence use runtime aliases. A dead, hostile, absent or displaced contact is acknowledged through a surviving witness, recovered statement or existing record; a replacement never pretends the original person survived. Quest-critical objects cannot be permanently destroyed before required use. Missed conversations, early discoveries and skipped quest givers are reconciled on quest load or area entry. Facts record knowledge, promises, custody, allegiance, injury, death and public exposure. Facts affect presentation and the world, but do not create a separate progression branch for every action.
				The 30 main quests follow the chronology below, each requiring its predecessor. Evidence found early is recorded; arrival triggers and alias recovery never bypass that requirement or ARRIVAL's explicit acceptance. Each quest resolves its immediate conflict, shows what changed and carries at least one understandable consequence forward. Failure, betrayal and abandonment change the situation where relevant without silently blocking the whole campaign. Fixed tragedies remain fixed when they are the point of a story. Agency concerns responsibility, evidence, cost, dignity and aftermath, not erasing every loss.

				The Unwritten Ledger - overarching campaign:
					The campaign asks: How do you create balance in a world that has become fundamentally unbalanced? Ashenwrath's communities depend on one another, but power, protection and responsibility no longer reach the same people. Courts claim obedience while neglecting service; households preserve lives and knowledge while concealing abuses; roads and lights fail between otherwise functioning settlements. Balance means making those relationships workable without demanding that the least powerful absorb every cost. It is not equal approval of every claim, a midpoint between cruelty and care, or a return to an innocent past. The old Threefold Compact was a practical agreement among early settlements: Sun-kept public beacons and visible records; Moon-kept household registers, contracts and stores; and the roads, ferries, watchtowers and ordinary people who maintained the spaces between them. Fog was never a third god or a signatory. It was the danger that grew where lights, records and obligations failed. The Unlit Lantern at Dimwater was the compact's highest signal station, not a device that could command or abolish Fog. Its three parts describe obligations, not the number of rulers. Valgard supplied fittings and sworn contracts; Hojimo's keepers counted the road workers; Harlette maintained the western food and willow route; Camelot inherited an eastern escort obligation with the Sinclair grant.
					Archduke Artemios is the principal human antagonist. As a young officer he witnessed a real Fog disaster worsened by rival courts disputing jurisdiction, cost and religious authority. He concluded that only one central power could coordinate the beacon network. To him, balance means a dependable order in which no local refusal can endanger everyone else. His diagnosis contains truth; his answer removes the checks that could keep the coordinating power from becoming the next source of harm. Over years he has withheld or altered selected dispatches, cultivated rivalries, acquired records and made each court depend on Severian arbitration. He did not cause every monster, murder, family feud or local calamity. He exploits existing wounds and turns some evidence toward his political purpose. His choices grow more desperate as the old compact becomes visible.
					The player is not a secret heir, chosen vessel or prophesied ruler. Their origin is genuinely unknown. During ARRIVAL, Bram enters the player's chosen name in Loxford's gate roll but leaves the place-of-origin column blank. Duke Donald gives the player one half of an ordinary split hazel tally as a receipt for stores and work; its matching half remains in the castle accounts. Its three-notch mark is a human record-keeping device, not a magical key. The player becomes important by travelling between people who distrust one another, doing practical work and deciding what to reveal, protect, repair or leave unresolved. Their influence grows from those acts and their consequences, not from a special right to decide everyone else's future.
					The campaign grows from keeping a hearth, pasture and watchtower working into disputes over family, justice and belonging; then into the limits of public truth, private protection and shared authority. Early repairs show what people need from one another. Personal conflicts show why restoring an old arrangement can restore its injustices. Later failures test whether cooperation can survive betrayal, uncertainty and unequal power. The final choice is whether to renew a shared compact with local safeguards, accept a limited imperial compact with enforceable checks, or refuse the old system and maintain modest local lights. Each offers a different way to distribute responsibility and a different risk of renewed imbalance. None abolishes Fog, recovers every loss or makes one ruler an unquestionable savior.
					The question recurs through each quest's existing conflict and aftermath, rather than a repeated speech or an added objective. Sun revelation can make power answerable or expose someone who needs shelter; Moon preservation can protect a life or shield an abuser. Neither faith is inherently the answer. Fog remains the existing danger of failed definition, not a third force to cultivate in equal measure. Balance is an ongoing responsibility, expressed through the existing choices, facts and world responses; it adds no alignment score, resource or new mechanic.

The player may question him about the missing information, but neither Bram nor anyone else knows where they came from. Hadwin opens a bank account on the keep's top floor and explains storage. 		Side Quests:
			Structure:
				Side quests are optional activities that expand the world beyond the main storyline. They develop NPCs, local communities, occupations, factions, regional conflicts, histories, and environmental storytelling. They give the player reasons to explore, interact with the world, discover secrets, and become invested in places and people that are not necessarily central to the main narrative. Side quests are not skill tutorials and do not award stand alone completion XP (but can still gain XP from skill related tasks). They should generally assume the player has already learned the relevant skills and mechanics through the main storyline or ordinary gameplay. Side quests may require the player to use existing skills, knowledge, equipment, or combat abilities, but they should not introduce core skill mechanics, provide formal skill instruction, or reward skill XP for completion. Any XP earned from ordinary gameplay actions remains governed by the game's normal skill progression system and is not a side-quest reward. Side quests should provide rewards that change what the player owns, knows, can access, or can do in the world rather than directly increasing skill levels. Rewards may include:
					Unique items and equipment
					Tools and useful supplies
					Crafting materials and resources
					Currency and valuable goods
					Access to buildings, services, shops, or restricted locations
					Access to shortcuts, routes, hidden areas, and new activities
					Merchant discounts, new stock, or special trading opportunities
					Information, secrets, documents, and lore
					Reputation, recognition, favors, and improved relationships with NPCs
					Keys, permissions, and other forms of practical access
				Usual Formula:
					1. Gather, process, deliver. This is the base loop of many side quests. An NPC states a need, the player collects inputs across the map, and returns them. The main design variables are item count, whether a processing step sits between gathering and delivery, how scattered the sources are, and what narrative or practical reward is provided. These quests should rely on existing gathering and processing mechanics rather than function as skill tutorials.
					2. The relay. The side quest is normally a chain of NPCs, each directing the player to another person, location, or object. The challenge is discovering who or what comes next. The primary purpose is to develop NPC relationships, reveal local stories, and guide the player through parts of the map they might otherwise overlook.
					3. The drop hunt. The quest asks the player to obtain an item carried or dropped by an enemy. It uses the existing loot system and RNG to control pacing. The purpose is to provide a narrative reason to engage with enemies and reward the player with useful items, resources, access, or information. It should assume the player already understands basic combat and loot mechanics.
					4. The deduction or puzzle. These quests require reasoning rather than errands. Players read clues, interpret evidence, sequence levers and item dependencies, or piece together information to discover a solution. The reward should reflect the discovery, such as a hidden item, access to a secret location, valuable information, or an otherwise unavailable opportunity.
					5. The reward as design intent. Side quests are designed around what the player receives or unlocks, not around skill XP or skill progression. Their rewards should provide tangible utility, meaningful choices, access, discovery, or narrative payoff. A side quest should leave the player with something they did not have before, whether that is a unique item, a useful service, a new route, a relationship, or knowledge of the world. Side quests never award Cooking XP, Crafting XP, Faith XP, Magic XP, Mining XP, or any other skill XP as a quest reward.

LORE:
    WORLD, ERA AND HISTORICAL CANON:

        Ashenwrath is the continent. SHADOWVEIL is the playable region in which the histories of seven settled powers meet: Loxford, Azharad, Severia, Valgard, Gorrick, Harlette and Camelot. The game begins on Solday, May 5th, 500 CE, with the player in Loxford Castle.

        Ashenwrath has been saved many times. Every rescue left somebody with a privilege their descendants now mistake for a natural right. Its history is shaped by migration, faith, marriage, inheritance, conquest, commerce, disease, craftsmanship and the labor required to maintain a settled life. No dynasty, religion or people possesses an uninterrupted claim to moral superiority.

        Year 500 CE represents a late-medieval civilization broadly comparable to the real world's year 1490, with deliberately amalgamated technology and institutions. Severia combines Roman civic ancestry with the wealth, art, diplomacy and administration of a flourishing Byzantine empire. Other societies retain older forms of household rule while using sophisticated imported tools. Dates are not produced by subtracting 990 from real-world dates.

        The seven present powers did not exist unchanged for thousands of years. Their settlements stand over older villages, estates, sanctuaries, ports and fortifications. Boundaries moved; families intermarried; rulers borrowed titles; conquerors adopted the institutions of the defeated. A surviving place-name does not establish an uninterrupted kingdom or bloodline.

        The present map is a compact representation of these historical relationships. The ocean beyond it is not additional playable territory. Historical voyages, foreign texts and overseas commerce establish a wider world without requiring another playable kingdom, travel system or active off-map imperial province. Population and army sizes are not fixed at totals that contradict the farms, beds, roads and supply capacity of the playable settlements.

        This section distinguishes established historical events from disputed accounts within the world. It is canon that certain legends are disputed. A character's claim, an inscription or a royal genealogy does not automatically become objective truth. Where an event remains unresolved below, its uncertainty is intentional and must survive later retellings.

    THE CALENDAR:

        CE means Common Era. Sun clergy also call it the Covenant Era. BCE means Before the Common Era. There is no year zero: 1 BCE is immediately followed by 1 CE.

        The common reckoning was standardized in 164 CE during the reign of Empress Ilyra. Severian clerks, western sanctuaries and merchant representatives agreed to date common contracts from the traditional birth year of Jaehaerys. The year begins in January even though Jaehaerys's traditional birthday falls on December 31st, the last day of 1 CE.

        The days of the week, in order, are Solday, Lunday, Marday, Merday, Iovday, Highday and Sterday. Solday, May 5th, 500 CE is the authoritative opening-date anchor. Familiar month names are the common translation of the local calendar. Earth weekday calculations do not override this anchor.

        Older records also use a ruler's regnal years, household calendars, harvest counts and local religious reckonings. Translating between these systems can produce genuine disagreement. An incorrect date does not necessarily mean a document is forged.

        Reign dates below identify accession and the end of recognized rule. A shared boundary year means one reign ended and another began during that year. Overlapping reigns are explicitly identified as rival claims. A dynasty's date range describes a sequence of rulers, not one person's lifespan. Where ancient succession is incomplete, the record says so rather than silently extending a ruler's life across the gap.

    SUN, MOON AND FOG:

        Sun worship and Moon worship both predate their best-known mortal teachers. Jaehaerys reformed an ancient public faith. Amyd reformed an ancient household faith. Neither created the first Sun or Moon practice, and neither was the heavenly power itself.

        SUN:

            The Sun is one unblinking light. It saves by seeing. Its deepest promise is that a life will be acknowledged and wrongdoing can be brought into the open. The Sun covenant values witnessed obligations, confession, honest measures, public judgment, named burials, charitable care and recognition of the dead.

            Jaehaerys was traditionally born on December 31st, 1 CE. His securely remembered public teaching belongs to 27-36 CE. He taught that a nobleman's oath and a laborer's wage were both fit to stand in the light. Public recognition imposed duties upon the powerful as well as the vulnerable. Traditions disagree about his last years and death; no universally established account of martyrdom or resurrection settles the dispute.

            The Sun Church is one recognized religious body with regional customs and rival interpretations. Valgard holds Jaehaerys's principal sanctuary. Its legalists emphasize sworn accountability. Loxford's customary clergy emphasize parish continuity and inherited obligation. Severian civic clergy emphasize order and practical coexistence. Hojimo's ministers emphasize recognition without pedigree. Camelot's chaplains emphasize hospitality, service and personal vows. These tendencies are not separate gods or wholly separate religions.

            The words "sin is being hidden" are doctrine interpreted by human beings, not proof that all privacy is wicked. Some priests understand concealment as the refusal to acknowledge a wrong; others use it to justify invasive scrutiny. The Church's capacity for charity, courage and reform is real. Its capacity for coercion, rank worship and pious violence is also real.

            Tall sanctuaries, open courts, visible altars and elevated repositories express the value placed upon witness. Loxford's high castle bank belongs to this tradition. Buildings and rituals acquire practical and political functions alongside their religious meaning.

            Prayer: "Sun, see me, and let me be true."

        MOON:

            The Moon is one light with many veils. It saves by keeping. Its deepest promise is that what is entrusted to a household or keeper will not be abandoned. Moon practice values hospitality, recitation, careful transmission, private testimony, household memory, binding agreements, honest workmanship and care for the admitted guest.

            Amyd was born on June 28th, 430 CE. He began public teaching in 456 CE and died in 482 CE. The precise day of his first public address and the precise day of his death are not established. His reform is recent enough that elderly contemporaries remain alive in 500 CE.

            Amyd taught that custody creates duties. A household could not claim sacred protection for its private books while using secrecy to erase a guest's wages, a widow's claim or a dependent's rights. Hospitality was an obligation rather than a favor bestowed only when a patron felt generous. He strengthened a common language of accountable keeping within traditions already many centuries old.

            Moon scholarship, magic, temples, contract customs and commercial institutions existed before Amyd. His followers inherited and argued over older texts. Household keepers, textual scholars, working families and court patrons differ over who can authenticate his sayings and how his principles apply. These disagreements do not turn Moon society into one political faction.

            Moon's danger is allowing custody to become possession: "you will be kept" becoming "you may never leave." Privacy can protect a vulnerable witness or conceal a powerful family's abuse. Neither use defines the entire faith. "Dark" in the Moon spellbook names a domain of concealment and hidden things, not an evil moral alignment.

            Enclosed courtyards, protected archives, kitchens, workshops and sheltered guest spaces embody the faith. Making, mending, feeding and receiving travelers can be religious acts. Moon communities also exist outside Azharad, including Khalid's shrine within Severia.

            Prayer: "Moon, hide me, and let me be kept."

        FOG:

            The Fog is also called the Veil or the stars. It is not a third god, a fallen deity, a rival church or an intentional enemy of humanity. It occupies the uncertain spaces between what Sun and Moon define. There is no verified first sin that created it and no historical villain who released it upon an otherwise complete world.

            Fog can be dangerous without being morally evil. It can generate destructive failed forms, retain memories, preserve music, shelter an uncanny encounter or obscure a passage. A memory appearing within Fog is not necessarily a living person restored from death. Wonder and danger coexist.

            Fog-formed monsters and Fog-afflicted people are distinct. Existing monster forms arise from unstable boundaries and unravel when killed. Dracula retains identity, appetite, responsibility and agency despite his particular affliction. An unusual appearance, accusation or unexplained ancestry does not establish that someone is a monster.

            The traditional birth date assigned to Zes is January 1st, 1850 BCE. Accounts disagree over whether Zes was one guide, several remembered people, a title, an apparition or a name later attached to unrelated events. Zes is not an established third god, and there is no canonical organized Fog religion founded by Zes.

            "Necrotic" in the Fog spellbook describes its relationship to death and its traces. It does not make every practitioner wicked. Scholarship, funerary investigation and respectful attention to the dead can be honorable. Lore does not confer new resurrection, immortality or unrestricted time-travel abilities.

            Settled obligations, maintenance, practiced Faith and dependable shelter can resist Fog. Bureaucratic registration alone does not create a soul, and an unrecorded person is not morally or ontologically inferior. A community can fulfill its duties and still encounter overwhelming danger. Suffering never proves that its victims lacked faith.

    THE WORLD BEFORE CROWNS - BEFORE APPROXIMATELY 2400 BCE:

        Shell heaps, worked flints, hearth circles and graves survive beneath later settlements. Ashenwrath was inhabited before its famous arrivals. Early communities moved between coastal fisheries, the Mere, western ridges, river valleys and the sheltered waters around Dimwater.

        Their practices do not divide neatly into modern Sun and Moon categories. They exposed some dead to daylight, wrapped others in household cloth, lit fires for returning travelers and left covered bowls for the absent. Later religious writers claim these customs as evidence of their own priority. Neither can prove exclusive descent.

        A tended hearth, a known path and people expecting one's return made survival more dependable. These habits preceded the formal doctrines that later explained them. The oldest tales describe the Veil as a place of loss, concealment and possibility. Some end with a lost child finding shelter where none stood in daylight; others end with nobody returning.

        No ruler of this era is securely identified. Later king lists that begin with a single lord of all Ashenwrath are retrospective genealogies. Standing stones, reused hearthstones, prehistoric tools and paths that ignore later borders are its surviving monuments.

    THE LANDINGS AND THE AGE OF WELLS - APPROXIMATELY 2400-1700 BCE:

        Brantor's crossing of the western sea is conventionally placed around 2400 BCE. The familiar account says he led refugees, that his companion Corin wrestled Gogmar and hurled him from the cliffs, and that the survivors founded New Trovyn near the modern Severian Harbor.

        Settlement layers support arrivals by sea and an early harbor community. They do not prove that Brantor found an empty continent, that ancient coastal "giants" constituted a separate species, or that any present imperial house descends from him. The surviving reliefs remain ambiguous. Brantor's exact lifespan, accession and death are not securely known; later lists calling him the first king are not reliable reign registers.

        New Trovyn prospered by storing fish, grain, timber and cargo between voyages. Houses entrusted with provisions turned temporary custody into hereditary privilege. Some married into earlier communities; others displaced inhabitants from good landing places. The same founding feast could commemorate rescue for one household and dispossession for another.

        Between approximately 2150 and 1750 BCE, southeastern well settlements developed into storehouse courts. Water, caravan shelter, grain and skilled work supported hereditary keepers. Hospitality could provide real protection while also requiring payment to whoever controlled the only dependable spring. Western communities supplied quarry stone and metal; lake families controlled ferries and reed passages.

        The tradition of Zes is assigned to this era. Its contradictory stories include a guide returning lost children, someone refusing a ruler's imposed name and a voice heard from an empty camp. Their common concern is the boundary between being kept, being claimed and being lost.

        Early vessel images and three-mark signs appear in several harbor and road traditions. Their resemblance to later Compact symbols suggests inheritance and reuse but does not prove an unchanged institution lasting three millennia.

    THE BROKEN CITIES - APPROXIMATELY 1700-1000 BCE:

        Repeated harvest failures, disrupted shipping, conflict over metal supplies, exhausted wells and local ashfalls broke older commercial networks. Collapse occurred unevenly across generations. Some courts burned; others lost population or changed rulers. Knowledge survived in families after the institutions supporting it disappeared.

        Refugees carried weaving methods, recipes, burial practices, metalworking knowledge and dialects toward the Mere, the western valleys and the northern harbor. Ashenwrath's cultures were already mixed long before rulers praised themselves for welcoming outsiders.

        The City of Brass belongs to this age of fortified storehouses. Its exact abandonment date is unresolved. It was a Moon-kept city that sealed itself so completely that wealth and dead remained preserved while no living community endured. Its inscriptions condemn hoarding. The initial closure may have combined emergency protection with coercion; its outcome does not establish that all Moon keeping is corrupt.

        Musa's later expedition account describes brass vessels, a pointing rider, a concealed entrance and men lured from the walls. Its surviving copy does not securely establish Musa's reign chronology or identify his patron with a present dynasty. The ruins lie within the Cinder Fields beyond the Sandstone Cliffs. They are a contained historical site, not an eighth modern kingdom. The vessels hold Fog residue rather than wish-granting genies.

        Aurel ruled a small highland holdfast from 1268 to 1249 BCE. His authority ended when his community scattered and his chamber was sealed. His preserved Moon-bound sleep endured after the political office ceased to exist. His chamber, royal seal and bier survive beneath Gallows Tor. Roots and mineral growth gave rise to the story of a king whose beard grew into the mountain. His old title gives him no jurisdiction over Valgard or any present settlement.

        Iskandar ruled a conquering road dominion from 1124 to 1090 BCE. His major campaigns began in 1120 BCE. He joined hired coastal engineers, mountain guides, household troops and conquered supply stations into an expeditionary system. The Hollow Crags gate belongs to this military tradition, although later reconstruction prevents every surviving stone from being attributed to his reign.

        The gate channeled danger and supported warnings. It did not imprison Fog. Later romances name "Unclean Nations," Gog and Magog, where the oldest local marks depict existing Fog monsters. Other romances add an aerial journey, a descent beneath the sea and a search for the source of life. These are literary traditions, not confirmed feats or new gameplay systems.

        The Road-Successor Wars lasted from 1090 to 1061 BCE. Iskandar's commanders Marak, Selvar and Daryon fought over tolls, grain stations and the right to collect his former tribute. None inherited a stable universal crown. The Settlement of the Milestones in 1061 BCE recognized separate custodians along the routes. Iskandar's roads endured longer than his dynasty.

    THE FIRST THREEFOLD COMPACT - APPROXIMATELY 1000-680 BCE:

        Independent coastal houses, western workings, lake refuges, eastern waystations and southeastern storehouses recovered gradually. Their wealth depended on exchanges no single settlement could secure alone.

        The Long Winter, the traditional name for successive harsh seasons between approximately 780 and 752 BCE, exposed this dependence. A town could possess grain without carts while another had carts without a safe crossing. People died within reach of provisions because competing custodians refused responsibility for the journey between them.

        In 730 BCE, representatives of these communities concluded the earliest reconstructable Threefold Compact. Its principal surviving witness names are Nereth of the harbor houses, Vaela of the western workings, Safren of the well courts, Hessa of the lake refuges and Ordan of the eastern road households. They were delegates and custodians, not the rulers of the present seven powers. None received universal sovereignty from the agreement.

        The Compact bound three obligations:

            WITNESS: Keep public warnings, acknowledge deaths and debts, preserve visible claims and provide a hearing.

            KEEPING: Preserve supplies, contracts, private testimony, household continuity and knowledge entrusted to custody.

            PASSAGE: Maintain roads, crossings, ferries, escorts, watch stations and the people whose labor connected settlements.

        Older Sun and Moon traditions supplied distinct forms of public and household custody. Passage represented the work between them, not a third religion. Fog was neither god nor signatory.

        The Compact was never a perfect constitution. Wealthier communities could negotiate favorable assessments, unfree labor existed, some witnesses were excluded and obligations were unevenly enforced. Its enduring achievement was acknowledging that a keeper owed duties beyond the walls of the place being kept.

        Substantial Lantern construction began in 690 BCE. Later generations enlarged and repaired it. Its island position and elevation above Dimwater made it the highest shared signal station. Its warnings depended upon observers, fuel, roads, local lights and people prepared to respond. The Lantern could not command, imprison or abolish Fog.

        The vessel associated with the Compact preserves a record of shared custody and limits upon a single keeper. The three lights refer to responsibilities expressed through the settlement, not three gods or three ruling bloodlines. Surviving older symbols, original agreements and later redactions must be distinguished.

    THE FIRST SEVERIAN EMPIRE - 680-351 BCE:

        A northern harbor league developed near old New Trovyn after 680 BCE. It standardized weights, protected shipping and arbitrated cargo disputes. League offices were initially shared among powerful houses.

        The Grain Convoy War of 622-620 BCE began when rival coastal patrons seized supply ships and refused the league's judgments. Severan, the league's emergency commander, broke the blockade with paid crews and agreements guaranteeing grain to cooperating households. The settlement made his military and judicial command renewable without the old rotation of office. The imperial office is dated from 620 BCE.

        The name Severia spread from the dominant administration to the northern polity over subsequent generations. It was not the ancient name of every people or settlement later included within the empire.

        The early emperors paid troops, bought grain before shortages, recognized useful outsiders and made judgments enforceable beyond one household's territory. Expansion used force, marriage, debt, charters and negotiated surrender. Defeated houses sometimes retained homes and burial rights while surrendering toll income or independent jurisdiction. Others accepted the empire to escape worse neighbors.

        The Threefold Compact entered imperial administration. Duties once undertaken jointly were increasingly described as benefits supplied by the emperor. Original texts survived within later grants even as their political meaning was obscured.

        The Ore Road Wars of 556-548 BCE brought western workings into closer imperial dependence. Emperor Marcell defeated a coalition of toll-holding lords but could not operate their mines without local knowledge. The settlement confirmed mine households in their workings while imposing common measures, transport duties and imperial appeal rights.

        Between approximately 510 and 350 BCE, Severia reached its first great imperial high age. Durable roads, measured taxes, supply depots, stone public works and professional officials linked the coast to inland production. Provincial households sent children to the harbor for education, patronage or guarantees of obedience, often all three.

        Erveth, remembered as the Last Warden of Ashmaw, ruled an eastern marcher holding from 472 to 440 BCE. He was a local warrior-king associated with imperial frontier obligations, not an emperor of Severia. In 461 BCE he defeated a destructive Fog creature with a prepared company, maintained signals and support from neighboring communities. His last expedition in 440 BCE reached Ashmaw without comparable support, and he died confronting a Dragon. The identity of that Dragon cannot be equated with the present Ashmaw Dragon merely because the lair is the same.

        Imperial prosperity made hereditary privilege harder to control. Public contracts purchased marriages; marriages supplied commanders; commanders defended profitable offices. Communities could value imperial courts and resent imperial collectors at the same time.

        The Five Standards War began in 351 BCE after Emperor Valerius's death. Competing commanders and heirs claimed extraordinary authority, withheld provincial revenues and prolonged emergency taxation. Kassar held the harbor crown until 337 BCE but failed to restore inland obedience. Disease, revenue loss and local defections continued beyond his death. The war's main campaigns ended in 337 BCE; their political fragmentation lasted centuries.

    THE YEARS OF MANY CROWNS - 351 BCE-82 CE:

        Severia's northern shipping, archive traditions and coastal imperial claim survived. Direct control of much of the interior did not. There was no single day when civilization ended.

        Road commanders became hereditary lords. Agricultural households fortified their stores. Assemblies bargained with incoming fighters. Coastal raiders, hired soldiers, smiths and displaced families entered older communities. Some burned homes; others repaired mills and married into the households they had once threatened. Their descendants later claimed ancient ownership of the same lands.

        Loxford grew around the Mere and surrounding agricultural holdings. Its early "kings" usually governed clusters of halls rather than the present kingdom. Public fiscal obligations became personal duties owed to nearby protectors. Older roads endured as royal custom even where no king could maintain them reliably.

        Harlette developed as a protected agricultural estate along the western supply route. Its later countship inherited both imperial language and local agreements. This mixed legitimacy eventually allowed Loxford and Valgard to demand service without giving either an uncontested right to annex the valley.

        Hojimo emerged as a refuge for travelers, displaced households, road workers and survivors whose witnesses were dead. The earliest surviving fragments of its name rolls belong to approximately 160 BCE. Keeper Neren is remembered for recording a person's name without first demanding a respectable lineage. He was a custodian, not the founder of an independent royal dynasty.

        The Niblung household conflicts belong approximately to 220-180 BCE. Sigfrid, Krimhild and Hagan appear in competing accounts of martial service, marriage, treasure and betrayal. Sigfrid's Dragon-blood bath remains an explanation offered by legend for extraordinary resilience, not an established source of invulnerability. The hoard's recovery cannot settle every motive in the surviving accounts.

        Ogier's remembered campaigns fall roughly two centuries before the surviving Loxford record series beginning in 98 CE, placing the dates he gives near the late BCE period. His account of a short stay with Morgen conflicts with the interval represented by those records. The failure of her preserved hospitality lamp freed him, but whether chronology, the lamp or a forgetting-crown explains his perceived interval remains unresolved. He is neither Aurel nor Erveth.

        Jaehaerys taught during the later part of this divided age, in 27-36 CE. His followers tended injuries, reconciled disputes, named the dead and held open hearings. Rulers adopted the covenant from conviction, administrative usefulness, political rivalry or a mixture of motives. Conversion did not erase every older household practice.

        The great political question within the new Sun covenant was whether recognition genuinely bound the powerful. A priest could invoke Jaehaerys to defend a worker's petition; a lord could invoke the same faith to demand submission to inherited rank. The conflict became part of the faith's institutional history rather than proof that the teaching itself was fraudulent.

    THE NORTHERN RESTORATION - 82-200 CE:

        Theodra secured the Severian throne in 82 CE with support from coastal households, commanders and revenue officials exhausted by competing military governments. Her restoration campaigns continued until 91 CE. She recovered selected depots and crossings while negotiating with inland rulers rather than attempting to reconquer every holding.

        The settlement of 96 CE reaffirmed older Threefold duties in the language of the growing Sun covenant. This redaction is the reason some surviving Compact copies sound younger than the obligations and artifacts they describe. The original Compact, later religious interpretation and current enforcement are separate historical layers.

        Theodra recodified judgments, stabilized customs and restored the usefulness of imperial arbitration. She supported Sun public institutions while confirming protected household custody for older Moon communities. Protection carried taxes and limits, and could be abused or withdrawn, but mixed communities had real reasons to defend the settlement.

        In 98 CE, the Loxford halls recognized Eadric as the holder of a common crown with defined harvest and defense obligations. The surviving continuous local record series begins with his accession. Loxford existed before this archive; the archive's starting date does not date its first settlement.

        The Bread Rising of 118 CE followed a failed Severian grain contract. Hungry crowds and rival court factions seized the public square. Theodra's troops recovered it with substantial civilian loss. Relief and rebuilding were funded partly by confiscating the fortunes of those blamed for the shortage. Later chronicles praise the ruler who restored bread; family laments remember those her soldiers killed. Both preserve real parts of her reign.

        Valgard's sanctuary drew pilgrims, donations and petitions to the western ore road. The Temple Knights received an enduring commission in 128 CE under Emperor Acast and the sanctuary's custodians. Their duty was to defend sanctuaries, relics, protected records and people entrusted to those spaces.

        In 143 CE, mine households, sanctuary representatives and local lords recognized Edmar as King of Valgard. His crown joined existing communities under a public guarantee of measures, contracts and road protection. It did not make him the sovereign of every Sun believer.

        The common calendar was standardized in 164 CE during Empress Ilyra's reign. Merchant participation and practical conversion between private calendars allowed adoption without requiring every household to surrender its own religious reckoning.

        Queen Selyne of Valgard commissioned the White Knights in 173 CE. Their public charge covered roads, open courts and the enforcement of recognized judgments. Their obligations overlap with those of the Temple Knights but are not identical. A Temple Knight defending sanctuary custody can oppose a White Knight executing a public warrant without either abandoning the Sun.

        The Valgard crown depended upon guild advances, competent smiths, transport and trained labor. The ability to summon troops did not supply good steel by itself. Guild rights arose from negotiated service, and later kings inherited both their benefits and their restrictions.

    THE WARS OVER RIGHTFUL LIGHT - 200-330 CE:

        The War of the Shuttered Shrines lasted from 214 to 229 CE. Emperor Damas's ministers ordered inventories of religious holdings and removed sacred images they accused of concealing wealth and encouraging corrupt devotion. The reformers included sincere critics of abuse and officials seeking revenue. Their opponents included sincere defenders of worship and families guarding profitable exemptions.

        Sanctuary custodians, local lords and affected communities resisted. Valgard's King Halden supported protection for sacred records while refusing to recognize every religious exemption as lawful. Several Moon households sheltered threatened objects and witnesses. Faith did not determine every allegiance.

        Damas died in 223 CE. Empress Selene ended the war through the Settlement of Open Images in 229 CE. Images were recognized as legitimate instruments of teaching and remembrance, several seizures were reversed, and some holdings remained subject to audit. Neither side obtained everything it demanded. Later partisan chronicles concealed these compromises.

        Blank faces in reliefs, replacement mosaics and religious vessels preserved far from their original shrines are physical remains of this conflict. It was principally a struggle inside Sun society rather than an inevitable Sun-Moon war.

        The War of the Mere Levies lasted from 238 to 247 CE. King Roderic of Loxford attempted to convert temporary military demands into permanent dues. Manor lords resisted, grain merchants withheld deliveries and clergy disagreed over whether the crown had violated its own oath. Smaller households suffered requisitions from both sides.

        The Charter of the Mere was accepted in 247 CE. It required consultation over certain exceptional levies and confirmed selected household and market rights. Roderic abdicated under pressure, and his brother Oswald received recognition after swearing to observe it. The charter did not free every tenant or grant equal political participation. Its beneficiaries remained concentrated among established households.

        Loxford's later boast of "ancient liberty" descends from this compromise. Some provisions defend people against arbitrary power; others protect existing privilege. Disputes over who qualifies for a right are as old as the right itself.

        During these centuries, southeastern Moon communities supported translations of coastal medicine, foreign mathematics, legal commentaries, poetry and navigation lore. Scholars corrected inherited texts as well as copied them. Their work predates Amyd's birth by generations. Learning moved with travelers and commerce rather than belonging exclusively to one court.

        The story of Sundar and Sumak belongs to the distant historical realm of Malinor in the third century CE. Sundar's exile, return and defeat of Sumak became known in Ashenwrath through merchant and household copies. Malinor was not an earlier name for Azharad and establishes no present local territorial claim. The recovered final page concerns Sundar's treatment of defeated houses and the limits imposed after victory. His exact reign cannot be reconstructed from the damaged surviving chronicle.

        Gerbert's documented working life falls approximately between 300 and 330 CE. The oldest components attributed to his brazen head belong to this period. Repairs, substituted fittings and later log entries explain information from subsequent generations. The mechanism is a historical instrument affected by Fog residue, not an omniscient intelligence. Its statements require comparison with independent evidence.

    THE WAR OF THE THREE CORONATIONS - 337-346 CE:

        Emperor Orest died in 337 CE without a succession accepted by every commander and major household. Dorion secured recognition from much of the harbor coalition. Leont received coronation through a rival palace and inland faction. Akran claimed the throne in 338 CE with support from unpaid military companies.

        Dorion promised stable customs and protection of established commercial rights. Leont promised western supporters repayment from confiscated coastal fortunes. Akran promised soldiers their arrears and denounced both rival coronations as purchases by wealthy patrons. Each possessed supporters with real grievances as well as ambitions of their own.

        The Siege of the Harbor in 342 CE brought the conflict to its most dangerous point. Waterfront districts burned, cargo was seized and civilians were displaced. A coalition of workers, shipping households and commanders maintained the harbor defenses and prevented a general sack. Leont died during the siege. Akran continued fighting until his defeat and exile in 345 CE.

        The Settlement of the Quays in 346 CE recognized Dorion as the sole emperor. Inland rulers retained broad independence in return for specified commercial and passage obligations. The emperor kept a title broader than his direct territorial command. Severia's fleet, customs administration and archive network survived substantially intact.

        The prevention of a full metropolitan sack shaped the following centuries. Severia retained the institutions needed to recover wealth and negotiate concessions back from powerful patrons. Its later strength was built upon survival and repair rather than an uninterrupted sequence of victories.

        Valgard's King Godmar died in the war's opening year after refusing unrestricted requisitions. His successor Odrik armed local defenses and negotiated limited service to the eventual settlement. Loxford's King Berengar furnished supplies selectively, avoiding complete subordination to any claimant. Their caution protected autonomy while prolonging uncertainty for communities along the contested roads.

    THE EMPTY SUMMER AND THE ARMSTRONG SETTLEMENT - 361-410 CE:

        The Empty Summer was an epidemic lasting from 361 to 365 CE, named after its first terrible harvest season. It spread through ports, fairs, work camps and households. It was a disease, not a Fog creature, a divine punishment or an attack organized by another faith.

        Neglected paths and abandoned homes became more vulnerable to Fog as care and maintenance failed. Crowded and devout communities also suffered, defeating claims that correct ritual alone guaranteed safety. Sun clergy and Moon keepers both organized relief. Some authorities scapegoated strangers; others defended them at personal risk. Merchants sometimes concealed outbreaks, and landlords sometimes prevented workers from leaving until flight was impossible.

        Mortality left empty houses, disputed plots and shortages of skilled labor. Survivors demanded higher wages and greater freedom of movement. Some employers attempted to enforce older service requirements more harshly. Queen Helva of Valgard funded food relief and temporary wage protections while struggling to preserve mine output. Queen Aveline of Loxford confirmed many abandoned tenancies to surviving kin but left numerous dependency claims unresolved.

        The Wage Risings of 373-376 CE began when western workers resisted demands that their children inherit unpaid service obligations. Some local settlements reduced duties; other protests ended in executions. Gallows Heath became a political warning as well as a place associated with Fog. The crown's later accounts emphasized restored order and omitted many names of the condemned.

        The Armstrong Succession War lasted from 382 to 389 CE. King Pelric's disputed levies and uncertain succession divided Loxford's major houses. Garric Armstrong, a military patron married to Elswyn, a daughter of the earlier Queen Aveline, gained support from landholders who demanded a reaffirmation of the Charter of the Mere. Pelric's supporters denied that marriage alone justified rebellion; they were correct that the marriage did not itself settle the crown.

        Garric prevailed through armed victories, negotiated defections and a final recognition assembly in 389 CE. Pelric died during the last campaign. Garric confirmed the charter, recognized selected household privileges and punished opponents. Court histories stress marriage and lawful recognition; hostile histories stress dispossession and executions.

        The Armstrong crown is a valid political settlement with obligations, not a timeless divine entitlement. Later evidence can challenge a property claim or expose a crime without automatically voiding every act of government since 389 CE.

        Harlette escaped direct annexation during these struggles because competing powers preferred a dependable supply route to granting a rival uncontested possession of it. Count Mircea maintained the estate until 398 CE. His son Dracula inherited its responsibilities and privileges that year.

    HARLETTE'S ABSENT LORD - 398-453 CE:

        Dracula was born on February 9th, 369 CE. He inherited Harlette in 398 CE and departed in 409 CE on a prolonged journey whose complete itinerary is unknown. He returned in 453 CE apparently unchanged while surviving retainers had aged forty-four years.

        During his absence, stewards governed, collected rents and negotiated passage. Neighboring rulers treated Harlette as a possible future acquisition, but none established uncontested sovereignty. Dracula's return restored personal enforcement and prevented an imminent division of estate rights among competing patrons.

        His Fog affliction sustains him on blood and binds his life to the manor's kept household. Its full acquisition history remains unknown. The condition neither absolves his choices nor makes his tenants nonhuman. Harlette remains an ordinary working human village with safe daytime fields and a real need for protection.

        Sealed blood agreements became a regular part of manor administration after his return. Their written terms are presented as private bargains. A tenant threatened with hunger or eviction often lacks meaningful freedom to refuse. Dracula's protection is real; so is the predation within his arrangements. A forged extension to an agreement does not excuse the coercion of the original demand.

    AZHARAD'S CONSOLIDATION AND AMYD'S REFORM - 421-482 CE:

        In 421 CE, oasis households agreed upon common arbitration for water, shelter, workshop disputes and caravan obligations. Rahman, a leading patron who could enforce the settlement, became the first recognized Sultan of the united Azharadi court. His accession joined older communities and wealth rather than founding civilization in an empty wilderness.

        The Open Weights settlement of 438 CE reduced overlapping internal tolls, guaranteed designated merchant lodging and recognized external contracts under agreed witnesses. Craftspeople and commercial households moved toward the oasis. Newcomers could gain prosperity and protection, but patronage remained unequal and workers did not share equally in the gains.

        Rahman's son Nasir succeeded in 447 CE. Under Nasir, palace arbitration expanded while old household custodians defended their independence. Amyd began teaching publicly in 456 CE. His demands for accountable keeping appealed to merchants seeking reliable contracts, workers seeking fair treatment and scholars concerned with the fidelity of transmission. Some patrons praised his hospitality teachings while resisting scrutiny of their own obligations.

        The War of the Southern Seals lasted from 462 to 469 CE. Disputes over water access, detained cargo, protected guests and competing claims to the southern crossing escalated into raids and armed seizures. Azharadi household companies faced a shifting collection of toll lords, private retainers and officials acting under incompatible warrants. Sun and Moon households appeared on both sides of individual disputes, and the conflict was never a unified religious crusade.

        Imperial patrol captains and western lords claimed to restore order while competing over who could levy payment for it. Merchants, working households and religious mediators forced negotiations when interrupted trade threatened their own survival. Nasir died in 469 CE. Saladin's accession settlement helped secure the Treaty of the Red Banks that year.

        The treaty recognized specified contracts under either Sun public witness or Moon household custody and renewed selected passage obligations. It did not grant Sun courts unrestricted inspection of Moon books or place every road under one ruler. This partial renewal preserved pieces of the old Compact without recovering its whole institutional meaning.

        Saladin strengthened the Sultanate while adopting much of Amyd's language. His household became known as Al-Saladin; the name denotes this ruling branch rather than a claim to ownership of the faith. Fahad, his son, inherited the court in 489 CE.

        Amyd died in 482 CE. Disputes over authentic sayings, trustworthy transmitters and relations between temple and palace intensified afterward. A living contemporary's memory has value but does not guarantee accuracy or disinterested interpretation. The faith is older than its reformer and larger than the dynasty using his prestige.

    SEVERIA'S LATER PROSPERITY:

        After the Three Coronations and the Empty Summer, Severia recovered through shipping, public repair, negotiated taxation and commercial agreements. The harbor remained indispensable without becoming morally entitled to govern every place dependent upon it.

        Theomar Laskaris took the throne in 423 CE after Empress Kassara's death. His marriage to her daughter and support from commanders and major coastal households established the new ruling house. His accession did not prove descent from Brantor or erase older coastal rights.

        By 470 CE, Severia supported flourishing public art, manuscripts, court craftsmanship, legal administration and charitable patronage. Imported dress and borrowed words were fashionable in some circles and resented in others. Cultural confidence coexisted with factional rivalries and sharp social inequality.

        Valgard's guild credit connected its mines to Azharadi workshops and Severian trade. Loxford's grain and timber purchased metal and imports. Harlette supplied its western route. Hojimo supported dangerous expeditions and preserved the names of their casualties. None could isolate itself without injuring its own people.

        Leonidas Laskaris inherited this system in 481 CE. He regards negotiated privilege as part of what allowed the empire to survive. Artemios increasingly regards the same privileges as obstacles preventing timely action. Their disagreement is about the limits of power within a functioning civilization, not whether civilization should exist.

    THE DISASTER AT DIMWATER AND THE FRAYING NETWORK - 474-500 CE:

        In 474 CE, a supply camp and dependent households at the Dimwater approaches were lost during a real Fog emergency. Patrol requests stalled while courts disputed jurisdiction, expense and religious authority. Some responders acted courageously; the combined effort still failed. The ruined camp lies within the established wilderness and is not an eighth settlement.

        Artemios, then a young officer of twenty, survived. The disaster convinced him that divided authority could kill people even when each institution claimed to fulfill its own law. His later diagnosis contains truth. His decision to remove meaningful consent and independent custody is his own responsibility.

        Emergency instructions suspended the common signal schedule in 476 CE pending a jurisdictional review. Individual offices continued portions of it using different timetables, accounts and chains of command. The oldest authorization survives through inconsistent copies. The identity and intent of the original order-giver are not established by the surviving administrative mark. Artemios must not be assigned authorship of that order merely because his later office held its copies.

        The network deteriorated gradually. Some beacons worked while connecting roads failed. Some crews were paid late; others lost materials or disputed whether a levy had already been collected. Local disputes, corruption, violence, accidents and ordinary shortages all contributed. The name Unlit Lantern describes prolonged unreliability rather than one universal instant when all protective magic ceased.

        Outside patrols to several Hojimo camps became unreliable and then ceased in 480 CE. Household assemblies and temporary captains organized their own defense. In 486 CE, Torkell united the camps under a common defensive assembly. Gorrick became the prevailing name for the town while Hojimo remained the monastery's name. Torkell's authority rests on continuing consent and performance, not an inherited ducal title.

        Between 493 and 499 CE, Artemios expanded his influence over dispatch custody, arbitration and emergency assistance. He altered or withheld selected reports, cultivated existing rivalries and offered imperial intervention on increasingly restrictive terms. He did not cause every crime, family betrayal, monster attack or failed harvest. His manipulation becomes more dangerous because real problems give it a plausible justification.

    ARTHUR AND THE FOUNDATION OF CAMELOT - 488-500 CE:

        Arthur was born on April 18th, 470 CE. His public sword-drawing in 488 CE became a sign around which supporters gathered. The act did not independently make him king, transfer the island or establish the player's destiny.

        In 489 CE, Arthur and his companions secured the eastern road through escort work, local agreements and armed defense. They acquired support because travelers and households received protection that competing patrons had failed to provide consistently.

        In 490 CE, Aldren and the Sinclair household granted Arthur the castle and attached lands. The deed preserved tenants' holdings, Sinclair family rooms and burial rights. Arthur accepted the inherited eastern escort obligation. Recognition by the attached households, sworn obligations and acknowledgment from neighboring powers established his crown within those bounds.

        Phillip recognized Arthur while expecting disciplined service. Torkell accepted escorts without accepting tribute. Severia offered resources with administrative conditions. Richard objected when asylum prevented recovery of household dependents. Dracula's claims over debtors presented another test of the new court's hospitality.

        Arthur married Guinevere in 492 CE. Her household administration and handling of petitions became essential to court life. Lancelot's ability and intimacy with the royal household made him indispensable and created a private fault line. The affair is real, but its public exposure and the subsequent fracture of the Round Table belong to the campaign rather than completed pre-game history.

        The Round Table offers a hearing without precedence. Its members nevertheless arrive with unequal wealth, incompatible obligations and older grievances. Their fellowship represents a sincere hope whose institutions are still young.

    THE OPENING YEAR - 500 CE:

        Richard has ruled Loxford since 484 CE. Phillip has ruled Valgard since 487 CE. Fahad has ruled Azharad since 489 CE. Leonidas has ruled Severia since 481 CE. Torkell has led Gorrick since 486 CE. Arthur has held Camelot's recognized crown since 490 CE. Dracula has held Harlette's countship since 398 CE, including the absence of 409-453 CE.

        Orwendil's death at the Barley Terraces occurred during the autumn harvest of 499 CE. Feng's seizure of stewardship and household rights is recent history. Amleth's subsequent investigation and confrontation remain events to be resolved during play.

        In early 500 CE, courts prepare seasonal obligations while repairs, supply problems and household grievances remain unsettled. Most people do not think they are living through the end of an age. They want wages, workable tools, healthy livestock, food, safety and a hearing when wronged.

        On Solday, May 5th, 500 CE, the player begins in Loxford Castle. The player's origin is genuinely unknown. Bram enters the chosen name in the gate roll and leaves the place-of-origin column blank. Donald's split hazel tally is a receipt for stores and work. Its three notches are a human accounting mark, not a magical key or proof of royal descent.

        The player becomes important by traveling between communities that distrust one another, preserving testimony and accepting responsibility for what to expose, protect, repair or leave unresolved. The central question is: How do you create balance in a world that has become fundamentally unbalanced? Inherited promises, their interpretation and the cost of broken obligations are recurring tests of that question; restoring an old arrangement is not automatically the same as making it just or workable.

    RULERS, DYNASTIES AND SUCCESSION:

        SEVERIA - THE EARLY IMPERIAL REGISTER:

            Severan, 620-589 BCE. Founder of the imperial office. Converted emergency convoy command into enduring authority after the Grain Convoy War. Established regular military pay and recognized commercial judgments across the harbor league.

            Orren, 589-557 BCE. Severan's son. Secured hereditary succession by renewing privileges to houses capable of challenging him. Extended storage and transport obligations without claiming direct ownership of every participating settlement.

            Marcell, 557-529 BCE. Orren's nephew, selected after Orren's sons failed to obtain sufficient recognition. Won the Ore Road Wars of 556-548 BCE. Confirmed local mine possession in exchange for common measures and imperial appeal rights.

            Nerissa, 529-501 BCE. Marcell's daughter. Recognized through the support of coastal courts and military paymasters. Reduced several wartime assessments, restored depots and used marriage agreements to bind competing houses to the throne.

            Aster, 501-468 BCE. Nerissa's son. Expanded public works and professional administration. His reign marked the consolidation of the first imperial high age; its expense increased reliance on wealthy contractors.

            Lysand, 468-439 BCE. Aster's younger son, succeeding after his elder brother died before accession. Regularized frontier service grants. Erveth's final expedition occurred during his reign, but Erveth's local crown was not the Severian throne.

            Cassian, 439-411 BCE. Lysand's cousin. Preserved succession through an agreement with commanders and coastal households. Enlarged legal archives while granting exemptions that strengthened several hereditary patrons.

            Sabren, 411-382 BCE. Cassian's son. Pursued costly frontier commitments. Used offices and tax assignments to satisfy military households, deepening the connection between public duty and private wealth.

            Valerius, 382-351 BCE. Sabren's son. Attempted to recover revenue from inherited exemptions. Died without a successor acceptable to the main military and household blocs, precipitating the Five Standards War.

            Kassar, 351-337 BCE. Valerius's nephew and the claimant holding the northern harbor. Retained the imperial title but failed to recover the interior. His death ended the principal phase of the war, not the fragmentation it had produced.

        SEVERIA - THE FRAGMENTED COASTAL SUCCESSIONS, 337 BCE-82 CE:

            The coastal imperial office survived, but several early succession rolls are incomplete or partisan. The empire did not become wholly empty of rulers during this period, and no single emperor ruled across the entire interval.

            The Varentine coastal house, 337-241 BCE. Founded by Varent, who ruled from 337 to 309 BCE after securing the harbor payroll and recognition of the principal quays. His successors maintained coastal administration while conceding wide inland independence. Several short reigns are not securely ordered in the surviving copies.

            The Ceryn house, 241-127 BCE. Established through marriage, creditor support and a negotiated transfer of the crown. Ceryna, its first ruler, reigned from 241 to 213 BCE. She reopened several commercial agreements; later rulers struggled with military debt. The dynasty's limits are known more securely than every individual accession.

            The Orass house, 127 BCE-18 CE. Orass ruled from 127 to 96 BCE after a military-backed succession. The house relied upon coastal garrisons and inherited commercial courts. Its later rulers remained emperors in coastal documents but exercised inconsistent authority beyond the northern routes.

            Illen, 18-41 CE. A negotiated successor supported by commanders and senior households. Ruled during Jaehaerys's public teaching without issuing a general conversion of the empire. His court's concern was stability and revenue.

            Oskan, 41-63 CE. Illen's military successor. Continued coastal government but assigned more income directly to commanders, leaving fewer common funds for repair and arbitration.

            Hadran, 63-82 CE. Last emperor of the divided military settlement. Lost the support of major paymasters and commanders. Deposed during Theodra's accession; his removal did not establish a right for every later officer to seize the throne.

        SEVERIA - THE RESTORED IMPERIAL SUCCESSION, 82-500 CE:

            Theodra, 82-119 CE. Founded the restored court through force, recognized succession and negotiated obligations. Led the Northern Restoration, confirmed the Compact redaction of 96 and suppressed the Bread Rising of 118. Her public achievements and responsibility for the suppression are both part of her record.

            Acast, 119-145 CE. Theodra's son. Favored consolidation over further conquest. Confirmed sanctuary protections, accepted the Temple Knights' commission in 128 and recognized Valgard's crown in 143 within negotiated limits.

            Ilyra, 145-174 CE. Acast's daughter. Secured recognition from commanders and coastal houses. Sponsored the common reckoning in 164, improved agreement between regional measures and patronized both public repositories and protected household learning.

            Marovan, 174-201 CE. Ilyra's son. Extended commercial privileges to finance repairs and court patronage. His succession failed when competing relatives and officers disputed the cost of inherited concessions.

            Damas, 201-223 CE. Married into the restored imperial family and obtained the crown through military and creditor support. Founded the Ardent ruling branch. Began the inventories and sacred-image removals that led to the War of the Shuttered Shrines.

            Selene, 223-240 CE. Damas's daughter. Inherited a religious and fiscal conflict she could not simply repress into submission. Negotiated the Settlement of Open Images in 229 while retaining limited public audit rights.

            Theron, 240-269 CE. Selene's nephew and recognized heir. Rebuilt damaged commercial relationships, encouraged translation and preferred negotiated passage to attempts at universal religious jurisdiction.

            Nerian, 269-295 CE. Theron's son. Expanded the use of household sureties and commercial arbitration. His dependence on influential guarantors made some contracts more reliable while shielding well-connected defaulters.

            Evadne, 295-321 CE. Nerian's sister. Received recognition after rival heirs failed to settle their claims. Supported skilled workshops, manuscripts and civic works during Gerbert's working lifetime. Her patronage did not make the devices attributed to him infallible.

            Orest, 321-337 CE. Evadne's nephew. Preserved prosperity but left military arrears and an unresolved succession. His death triggered the War of the Three Coronations.

            Dorion, 337-369 CE. Claimed the crown in 337 with the harbor coalition; recognized as sole emperor in 346. Founded the Merovar settlement. Preserved the metropolitan core during the civil war and governed during the Empty Summer.

            Leont, rival claimant, 337-342 CE. Supported by a palace faction and inland patrons promised confiscated fortunes. Died during the Siege of the Harbor. His coronation was real, but his rule was never universally recognized.

            Akran, rival claimant, 338-345 CE. Supported by unpaid military companies. Defeated and exiled in 345. Never held uncontested sovereignty over Severia. His troops' unpaid wages remained a genuine grievance even after his claim failed.

            Basilan, 369-395 CE. Dorion's son. Rebuilt trade and revenues after the epidemic. Negotiated with stronger labor households while permitting local patrons to resist some reforms. Recognized the Armstrong settlement after Garric's accession in 389.

            Kassara, 395-423 CE. Basilan's daughter. Reduced selected inherited exemptions through repayment and agreement rather than wholesale confiscation. Her court cultivated commercial families whose cooperation later supported her son-in-law Theomar.

            Theomar Laskaris, 423-449 CE. Kassara's son-in-law. Established the Laskaris dynasty through recognition and marriage, not an invented unbroken descent from the first landing. Renewed harbor works and agreements with the southern court.

            Alexan Laskaris, 449-473 CE. Theomar's son. Expanded civic patronage and shipping protection. Authorized interventions during the War of the Southern Seals but accepted the limited Treaty of the Red Banks in 469.

            Ilarion Laskaris, 473-481 CE. Alexan's son and Leonidas's father. Reigned during the Dimwater disaster of 474 and the common schedule's suspension in 476. The occurrence of an order during his reign does not by itself identify which office drafted, distorted or enforced it.

            Leonidas Laskaris, 481-present. Born March 3rd, 448 CE; age 52 on the opening date. Succeeded his father with recognition from commanders and principal coastal households. Defends the negotiated privileges underpinning imperial prosperity, even when they frustrate consistent administration. Elyra is his daughter; her choices and relationships are not mere instruments of succession.

            Archduke Artemios is not a reigning emperor or a designated automatic successor. Born March 21st, 454 CE; age 46 on the opening date. His position combines court rank, administrative influence and experience in military service. His ambition is to bring beacons, road levies, dispatches and custody of records under a single imperial chain of command.

        LOXFORD - THE HOUSE OF THE MERE, 98-210 CE:

            Eadric, 98-128 CE. First ruler in the continuous surviving Loxford register. Joined the principal halls under a common crown with agreed harvest and defense obligations. Earlier local rulers existed but do not form a verified continuous succession.

            Harlon, 128-156 CE. Eadric's son. Confirmed parish boundaries and hereditary holdings while accepting specified outside judgments concerning trade.

            Maeric, 156-183 CE. Harlon's son. Encouraged woodland clearance and farm obligations. His grants increased production while making disputes over inherited labor more consequential.

            Edwyn, 183-210 CE. Maeric's brother. Succeeded after Maeric's heirs failed to secure sufficient support. Married his daughter into the Thornfield household to settle a rival claim.

        LOXFORD - THE THORNFIELD HOUSE, 210-389 CE:

            Aldwyn, 210-238 CE. Edwyn's grandson through his daughter and the first Thornfield king. Inherited recognition through marriage and agreement. Increased military demands during regional religious conflict.

            Roderic, 238-247 CE. Aldwyn's son. Attempted to make exceptional levies permanent. Lost the War of the Mere Levies and abdicated when the charter settlement became a condition of continued government.

            Oswald, 247-274 CE. Roderic's brother. Received the crown after swearing to the Charter of the Mere. His restraint preserved the settlement but left powerful households with lasting exemptions.

            Elswyth, 274-299 CE. Oswald's daughter. Consolidated markets and grain routes. Defended selected widow and inheritance claims while retaining the hierarchy governing household dependents.

            Godwyn, 299-326 CE. Elswyth's son. Supported mills, storehouses and public rites. His reign left prosperous estates whose private obligations were often better maintained than the shared road between them.

            Berengar, 326-350 CE. Godwyn's son. Avoided complete commitment to any claimant during the Three Coronations. Supplied favored partners while protecting Loxford's autonomy and tolerating opportunistic seizures by some retainers.

            Aveline, 350-375 CE. Berengar's sister. Reigned during the Empty Summer. Confirmed many surviving relatives in abandoned tenancies and authorized relief, but her government also enforced disputed service claims during the Wage Risings.

            Pelric, 375-389 CE. Aveline's nephew. Inherited unresolved labor disputes and a contested succession. His levies and patronage divided the great households. Died in the final campaign of the Armstrong Succession War.

        LOXFORD - THE ARMSTRONG HOUSE, 389-500 CE:

            Garric Armstrong, 389-416 CE. Husband of Elswyn, daughter of Queen Aveline. Won military and political recognition in 389. Renewed the charter, redistributed some defeated estates and established the terms later described as the ancient peace.

            Osbert Armstrong, 416-443 CE. Garric's son. Maintained the settlement through marriage alliances and confirmed manor privileges. Deferred some shared repairs to local patrons, strengthening their discretion.

            Halric Armstrong, 443-466 CE. Osbert's son. Developed trade with the rising Azharadi court while resisting external scrutiny of dependent households. Accepted Dracula's restored possession of Harlette in practice when passage and produce resumed.

            Alric Armstrong, 466-484 CE. Halric's son and Richard's father. Governed during the Red Banks settlement, Dimwater disaster and loss of common scheduling. Favored preserving local discretion over accepting broad imperial supervision.

            Richard Armstrong, 484-present. Born February 12th, 447 CE; age 53 on the opening date. Renewed the inherited peace and public Sun obligations. Depends on manor patronage, harvest contributions and the cooperation of men such as Earl William. His crown is lawful within its settlement, but its claims over dependents and inherited privileges are contestable.

        VALGARD - THE CROWN OF THE SANCTUARY ROAD, 143-500 CE:

            Edmar, 143-170 CE. First recognized King of Valgard. Bound mine households, local lords and sanctuary custodians through guarantees of measures, passage and public judgment. His crown did not absorb the Church itself.

            Selyne, 170-196 CE. Edmar's daughter. Commissioned the White Knights in 173 and confirmed the separate sanctuary duties of the Temple Knights. Made protection of public courts a royal obligation.

            Halden, 196-224 CE. Selyne's son. Defended sanctuary custody during the Shuttered Shrines conflict while resisting blanket immunity for every wealthy religious patron.

            Torvald, 224-252 CE. Halden's nephew. Accepted the Open Images settlement and secured recognition of Valgard's local courts. Refused to let Loxford's levy war establish unlimited demands on the ore road.

            Merovan, 252-279 CE. Torvald's son. Expanded assay requirements and guild obligations. Improved reliability of exports while granting masters privileges later used to restrict entry into trades.

            Adela, 279-307 CE. Merovan's daughter. Strengthened public petition procedures and recognized several injury awards. Enforcement still depended upon available funds and the cooperation of employers.

            Godmar, 307-337 CE. Adela's son. Resisted unrestricted imperial requisitions and died during the opening conflict of the Three Coronations.

            Odrik, 337-358 CE. Godmar's nephew. Fortified local approaches, negotiated limited military assistance and preserved the crown's autonomy in the Settlement of the Quays.

            Helva, 358-381 CE. Odrik's daughter. Governed during the Empty Summer and Wage Risings. Funded relief and temporary wage protections while allowing harsh enforcement in places where advisers claimed mine production was endangered.

            Berold, 381-405 CE. Helva's son. Restored trade after the risings. Recognized Garric's Loxford crown in return for renewed passage obligations rather than accepting Armstrong ownership of every western road.

            Ewald, 405-432 CE. Berold's son. Expanded guild lending for military supplies. Strengthened the crown materially while increasing its indebtedness to skilled households.

            Roderan, 432-458 CE. Ewald's son. Protected ore exports to Azharad and confirmed several mixed-custody contracts. Temple and public court claims remained distinct under his rule.

            Maerold, 458-487 CE. Roderan's son and Phillip's father. Reigned during the Southern Seals conflict and later coordination failures. Deferred disputed assessments to preserve guild support, leaving a complicated inheritance.

            Phillip, 487-present. Born January 17th, 454 CE; age 46 on the opening date. Seeks consistent enforcement of sworn obligations and recognizes Arthur's bounded crown. His armor supply and treasury depend upon guild advances and workers whose demands cannot be dismissed without weakening his own policy.

        AZHARAD - THE OASIS SULTANS, 421-500 CE:

            Rahman, 421-447 CE. First Sultan of the united oasis court. Secured recognition from existing households through water arbitration, route defense and patronage. Enacted the Open Weights settlement in 438.

            Nasir, 447-469 CE. Rahman's son. Expanded palace arbitration and inherited disputes with independent keepers. Reigned during the beginning of Amyd's public teaching and the War of the Southern Seals. Died before the final treaty was secured.

            Saladin, 469-489 CE. Nasir's son. Established the Al-Saladin ruling branch, concluded the Treaty of the Red Banks and strengthened the Sultanate's commercial standing. Honored Amyd's teaching while consolidating palace influence over which household judgments received enforcement.

            Fahad Al-Saladin, 489-present. Born April 8th, 452 CE; age 48 on the opening date. Saladin's son. Governs through patronage, kinship, learned advisers and household agreements. Defends Moon custody against compulsory Sun inspection while increasingly favoring the household accounts most useful to his court. Prince Jasaf's later decision to withdraw from immediate succession ambitions does not automatically transfer the crown or establish another kingdom.

        HARLETTE - THE COUNTAL SUCCESSION, 202-500 CE:

            Harlette's estate predates its formal countal register. In 202 CE, imperial recognition and local assent confirmed Vaudren as count in return for food, passage and protection duties. This grant preserved a distinct estate rather than making the valley part of a future Loxford or Valgard royal domain.

            Vaudren, 202-232 CE. First count in the continuous countal register. Formalized estate duties and tenant holdings. His descendants inherited obligations as well as rents.

            Mirevan, 232-260 CE. Vaudren's son. Kept the western food route working during Loxford's levy war. Accepted limited requisitions without surrendering the countship.

            Radovan, 260-289 CE. Mirevan's son. Repaired mills and formalized several inherited tenancies. Household accounts became a major source of practical authority.

            Ilona, 289-318 CE. Radovan's daughter. Defended tenant succession against competing relatives of the countal house while maintaining dues owed to the manor.

            Dragomir, 318-345 CE. Ilona's son. Reigned during the Three Coronations and negotiated passage with rival commanders. Died before the final imperial settlement.

            Vlastan, 345-369 CE. Dragomir's brother. Preserved the valley's separate estate status and governed through the Empty Summer. Some surviving holdings date their current terms to his emergency grants.

            Mircea, 369-398 CE. Vlastan's son and Dracula's father. Maintained produce and passage agreements through the Wage Risings and Armstrong succession. Avoided giving either neighboring crown an uncontested annexation claim.

            Dracula, 398-present. Born February 9th, 369 CE; age 131 on the opening date, although his appearance does not reflect ordinary aging. Absent from 409 to 453; countal rights administered by stewards rather than transferred to another sovereign. His return restored direct government and introduced the present predatory household arrangements.

            Othren, acting steward, 409-434 CE. Administered rents and protection during the first part of Dracula's absence. Never received the countship.

            Ravela, acting steward, 434-453 CE. Succeeded Othren in administration and defended the manor's surviving claims against neighboring patrons. Relinquished governing authority when Dracula returned. Varek's present service belongs to a later household administration and does not require him to have served throughout the absence.

        HOJIMO AND GORRICK - KEEPERS AND ASSEMBLY LEADERS:

            Hojimo was a refuge and custodial community before it was part of Gorrick. Its keepers were not an unbroken dynasty of territorial kings. Surviving early fragments name Neren around 160 BCE; most intermediate keeper successions are incomplete and are not used to fabricate a continuous royal pedigree.

            Astren, senior keeper, 438-461 CE. Maintained the name rolls and guest accommodations under increasingly inconsistent outside protection. Recognized both Sun rites and the value of Moon-kept testimony.

            Ragna, senior keeper, 461-480 CE. Preserved workers' and travelers' claims during the Southern Seals conflict and the later coordination failures. Her authority concerned custody and sanctuary rather than ownership of every surrounding camp.

            Father Eirik, principal religious custodian from 480 CE. Maintains the monastery's Sun rites, protected testimony and older rolls. His office is distinct from Torkell's civil and defensive leadership.

            Styr, rotating defense captain, 480-483 CE. Chosen by participating camps after several outside patrols ceased. Held a limited emergency commission rather than a crown.

            Yrven, rotating defense captain, 483-486 CE. Coordinated shared watches and supply contributions. Transferred the commission to the broader assembly settlement that recognized Torkell.

            Torkell, chieftain, 486-present. Born February 27th, 451 CE; age 49 on the opening date. United camp defense and household assemblies. His standing is reviewed through the assembly and depends on winter stores, fair shares and returning patrols. The title "Frontier Duke" describes an administrative role in NPC classification, not a hereditary ducal claim over Gorrick.

        CAMELOT - THE SINCLAIR ESTATE AND ARTHUR'S CROWN:

            The Sinclair household held the eastern castle estate before it became Camelot's royal seat. Its heads were estate custodians and lords, not earlier kings of Arthur's kingdom.

            Maelor, head of the Sinclair estate, 312-341 CE. Received formal confirmation of the castle lands and an eastern escort obligation. This confirmation preserved older local holdings rather than declaring the land previously empty.

            Edras, 341-368 CE. Maelor's son. Kept the estate through the final civil-war years and epidemic. Deferred expansion to preserve the household and its tenants.

            Alwen, 368-397 CE. Edras's daughter. Restored farms and revised several service agreements after population loss.

            Cedren, 397-424 CE. Alwen's son. Preserved independent custody of family records while buying protection for exposed road stretches.

            Osgar, 424-453 CE. Cedren's son. Improved the castle's service buildings and negotiated escort contributions with neighboring patrons.

            Hadren, 453-478 CE. Osgar's son. Governed during the Southern Seals conflict and Dimwater emergency. Died leaving an estate burdened by an escort duty it could not consistently fulfill alone.

            Aldren, estate head from 478 CE. Hadren's son. Held the castle directly until the grant to Arthur in 490. Continues as head of the retained Sinclair household and its protected interests; his family did not vanish when the royal court arrived.

            Arthur, King of Camelot, 490-present. Born April 18th, 470 CE; age 30 on the opening date. Public sword-drawing in 488; eastern road protection in 489; lawful grant and recognition in 490; marriage to Guinevere in 492. His legitimacy rests on service, recognition and continuing obligations within a specific grant.

            Guinevere is queen and manager of her own household and petitions, not a separate territorial sovereign. Lancelot, Gawain and Bedivere hold distinct knightly responsibilities. Merlin is an adviser and custodian of learning, not the hidden ruler of the kingdom. None of these offices confers automatic succession to Arthur's crown.

    WARS, REBELLIONS AND SETTLEMENTS - REFERENCE REGISTER:

        Iskandar's Road Campaigns, 1120-1090 BCE:
            Belligerents: Iskandar's expanding expeditionary dominion and successive local road, pass and storehouse rulers.
            Causes: Access to supplies, toll income and strategic movement.
            Result: Connected military routes, fortified bottlenecks and tribute relationships. The dominion fractured after Iskandar's death; its engineering survived in later use.

        Road-Successor Wars, 1090-1061 BCE:
            Belligerents: Marak, Selvar and Daryon with changing local allies.
            Causes: Division of Iskandar's tribute, stations and authority.
            Result: Settlement of the Milestones; separate custodians recognized. No successor acquired a permanent universal crown.

        Grain Convoy War, 622-620 BCE:
            Belligerents: The harbor league under Severan and rival patrons seizing its supply traffic.
            Causes: Grain access, enforcement of judgments and control of provisioning.
            Result: Blockade broken, emergency command made enduring, Severian imperial office established.

        Ore Road Wars, 556-548 BCE:
            Belligerents: Emperor Marcell and a coalition of western toll lords.
            Causes: Competing levies and access to metal supplies.
            Result: Imperial appeal and common measures accepted; local working rights retained. Future guild and crown disputes inherited both sides of this compromise.

        Five Standards War, 351-337 BCE:
            Belligerents: Kassar and four principal rival military-claimant coalitions; the coalitions' changing membership is better established than every claimant's personal chronology.
            Causes: Valerius's disputed succession, military pay and revenue exemptions.
            Result: Coastal imperial survival with extensive inland fragmentation. Northern institutions endured while many local rulers became effectively independent.

        Northern Restoration Campaigns, 82-91 CE:
            Belligerents: Theodra's restored court and commanders or local patrons refusing its early settlements.
            Causes: Recovery of selected depots, customs income and enforceable passage.
            Result: Limited military recovery followed by negotiated autonomy. The broader constitutional settlement continued through the Compact redaction of 96 and local recognitions afterward.

        Bread Rising, 118 CE:
            Belligerents: Hungry crowds and opportunistic court factions against Theodra's city forces.
            Causes: Failed grain provision, hardship and political rivalry.
            Result: Violent suppression, confiscations, food relief and rebuilding. The restoration of supply did not erase civilian deaths.

        War of the Shuttered Shrines, 214-229 CE:
            Belligerents: Imperial inventory and image-removal factions against sanctuary defenders and allied local powers; religious affiliation did not determine every alliance.
            Causes: Sacred images, custody, exemptions, genuine corruption and coveted revenue.
            Result: Settlement of Open Images under Selene. Religious images recognized, selected seizures reversed, limited audit powers retained.

        War of the Mere Levies, 238-247 CE:
            Belligerents: King Roderic against a coalition of Loxford lords, market interests and divided religious supporters.
            Causes: Conversion of extraordinary contributions into permanent dues.
            Result: Charter of the Mere; Roderic's abdication; Oswald's accession under oath. Privileges expanded for recognized beneficiaries without universal emancipation.

        War of the Three Coronations, 337-346 CE:
            Belligerents: Dorion, Leont and Akran with maritime, palace, inland and military supporters.
            Causes: Orest's succession, unpaid troops, confiscation promises and contested privileges.
            Result: Dorion recognized alone; Leont dead; Akran exiled; Settlement of the Quays. Harbor, fleet and core administration survived despite severe damage.

        Wage Risings, 373-376 CE:
            Belligerents: Local worker and dependent-household coalitions against assorted employers, lords and enforcing officials.
            Causes: Inherited service demands and attempts to suppress post-epidemic bargaining power.
            Result: Uneven wage and duty concessions, punishments and executions. No single all-continent rebel government or universal settlement emerged.

        Armstrong Succession War, 382-389 CE:
            Belligerents: Pelric's royal supporters and Garric Armstrong's alliance of claimant kin, landholders and military patrons.
            Causes: Disputed succession, levies, household rights and opportunity for dynastic advancement.
            Result: Pelric's death, Garric's recognition, renewed charter and redistribution of defeated holdings. Richard's present crown descends from this settlement.

        War of the Southern Seals, 462-469 CE:
            Belligerents: Azharadi household companies, independent toll patrons, private retainers and officials claiming incompatible enforcement rights; allegiances shifted by dispute.
            Causes: Water, detained cargo, guest protection, tolls and the southern crossing.
            Result: Treaty of the Red Banks, limited recognition across forms of custody and selected renewed passage duties. Neither religious uniformity nor universal imperial command was imposed.

        Dimwater emergency, 474 CE:
            Classification: A catastrophic failure of coordinated response, not a war against a new kingdom or deity.
            Result: Loss of a camp and households, survivors including Artemios, emergency administrative intervention and lasting distrust. The subsequent schedule suspension of 476 must be distinguished from the disaster itself.

        No general war among all seven present powers has begun by Solday, May 5th, 500 CE. Local violence, banditry, disputed levies, coercion and failing protection create the conditions for escalation. Campaign confrontations and possible settlements remain future events.

    THE SEVEN POWERS IN 500 CE:

        LOXFORD:

            Loxford's authority is landed, local, rank-conscious and sacramental. Its crown rests on holdings, harvest obligations, manor patronage and public Sun rites. Richard calls himself the guardian of an ancient peace because the Armstrong settlement is now old enough to feel permanent to ordinary households. Neglected roads and selective enforcement expose how unevenly that peace is maintained.

            Earl William's patronage, Osric's agricultural obligations, Donald's logistical accounts and Gregory's public religious authority represent different parts of the same settlement. Their interests often align, but none is merely an extension of the king's personal will. A manor may preserve its own buildings while disputing payment for the road serving everybody.

            Valgard supplies metal and demands serious enforcement of oaths. Harlette provides produce under a lord whose household practices trouble the Church. Azharadi trade and Severian records are useful even when outside judgment is resented. Arthur's asylum creates a direct challenge to claims over dependents.

            Loxford's identity includes harvest suppers, familiar bells, inherited craft knowledge, village games and pride in work done well. Its history is not only oppression. The problem is deciding which parts of inherited custom preserve a community and which preserve another person's power over it.

        AZHARAD:

            Azharad is urban, clannish, commercial and learned. Its households combine lineage, hospitality and textual authority. Prestige comes through poetry, argument, patronage, skilled production and the reliability of a promise. Palace arbitration can protect trade while deciding that a favored family's account deserves greater weight than a worker's.

            Fahad depends on household allies, Hassan's administration, workshop production and trusted custodians such as Idris. Rafiq and Yusuf represent teaching and interpretation that the palace cannot honestly claim to have invented. Bilal's withdrawal from court life reflects a dispute over how learning serves people, not the absence of learning from the court.

            Valgard ore supports Azharadi furnaces, Severian shipping carries goods, Gorrick buys frontier supplies and Camelot offers escorts with different political conditions. Fahad resists any compact granting a Sun court automatic access to Moon-kept household books. His objection can be principled even when some protected books conceal abuses.

            Courtyard meals, recitation contests, date gardens, meticulous leatherwork and arguments continued over food belong to ordinary life. Trade and migration have produced mixed customs rather than one timeless, uniform "desert culture."

        SEVERIA:

            Severia is the strongest regional center of administration, maritime commerce, public art and accumulated records. Its society rewards multilingual competence, family connections, negotiation and civic prestige. Public Sun institutions and visible Moon practice coexist through accommodation, bargaining and everyday familiarity.

            Leonidas governs through privileges whose holders can obstruct him but also make government possible. Artemios wants a more direct chain of command. Constantine's revenue interests, the Museum's pursuit of evidence, the archive's custodial power and harbor workers' dependence on open trade can produce different responses to the same dispute.

            Severia needs Loxford grain, Valgard metal and Azharadi manufactures. Its usefulness supplies influence, not a natural right to rule the suppliers. Coastal families absorbed through older charters can retain household rights without retaining independent sovereign crowns.

            Harbor festivities, public art, crowded kitchens, fine manuscripts and neighbors borrowing one another's customs give the empire a life worth preserving. Its history contains sincere service, political murder, clever compromise and preventable cruelty. No one description belongs to every Severian.

        VALGARD:

            Valgard joins sacred authority, public law and industrial work. A knight's oath, a mason's wage and a miner's injury claim are meant to bind those responsible. The crown's reputation depends upon making that principle credible.

            Phillip's effort to standardize enforcement conflicts with the exemptions and advances financing his own armories. The White Knights defend public courts and roads; the Temple Knights defend sanctuaries, relics and records. Neither order automatically possesses the superior claim in every dispute.

            Valgard buys grain and timber, exports metal and needs reliable transport. It recognizes Arthur's crown but distrusts a court where personal loyalty might outrank written duty. It welcomes Harlette's produce while demanding scrutiny of Dracula's household practices.

            Guild celebrations, admired masterworks, apprenticeship traditions and public recognition of competent service are central to local pride. Exhausted miners are people with households, grievances and expertise, not anonymous fuel for a knightly aesthetic.

        GORRICK:

            Gorrick's authority begins with household trust, stores and returning patrols. Outsiders can earn standing through work. A prestigious title without a useful contribution buys little. The assembly's ideals do not prevent insiders from forming a closed circle or failing an unpopular neighbor.

            Hojimo's name rolls preserve people omitted by courts concerned with ancestry. Its Sun rites coexist with older hospitality practices and a protected place for Moon-kept testimony. Torkell's civil leadership and Eirik's sanctuary duties are separate sources of authority that must cooperate.

            Gorrick sells repaired arms, charcoal and expedition supplies. It needs Valgard iron, Severian credit and trained escorts. Rejecting an outside garrison does not make economic independence possible. Closing the gates would hurt its own households before it humbled the larger courts.

            A returning expedition, a repaired roof, a good winter store and a fair distribution of difficult work are public achievements. The town's warmth matters as much as its frontier severity.

        HARLETTE:

            Harlette is a human agricultural village of inherited tenancies, strict hospitality, willow work and careful household memory. Dracula hears petitions and protects the road; those services explain why some tenants defend him despite the price of his rule.

            Loxford tolerates his position while passage remains dependable. Valgard's Temple Knights demand access to records that Dracula treats as private. Arthur's hospitality makes Camelot a possible mediator and an unwelcome refuge for debtors claimed by the manor.

            Ilva, Borin, Radek, Vesna and their neighbors have ordinary livelihoods. Sun worship continues in the chapel. Produce markets, shared meals and daytime gossip are real parts of the village. Darkness in the manor does not make every family complicit or turn the fields into high-danger monster ground.

        CAMELOT:

            Camelot is a small and recent royal court built on public promises and practical escort service. Its lawfulness rests upon a limited grant and recognized obligations. The Round Table's lack of formal precedence does not equalize the resources or influence of everyone seated there.

            Arthur's defense of dependents and defeated enemies gives his kingship substance. Guinevere's household work, Lancelot's service, Gawain's insistence on honor, Bedivere's escorts and Merlin's counsel also sustain it. Personal dependence is both its strength and its danger.

            The Sinclair family retains rights that emergency need does not automatically erase. Imperial aid carries administrative conditions; refusal carries practical costs. A court can mean its promise of refuge and still lack the resources to protect everybody who seeks it.

    LAW, INHERITANCE AND ORDINARY POWER:

        Sovereignty is not the same as possession of every right within a border. A crown, manor, sanctuary, guild and household may hold different claims over the same road, mill, burial ground or person. Disputes often concern which obligation takes precedence rather than which party owns everything.

        Marriage can transfer income, guarantee an alliance, settle an inheritance claim or bind a household to service. It does not erase the choices of the people married. A lawful marriage can be personally unhappy; a loving attachment can breach genuine obligations; neither condition settles every political consequence.

        Women have ruled crowns and estates throughout the recorded successions when inheritance, recognition and power supported their claims. This does not mean every institution offers equal access. A woman may possess a valid estate right while being excluded from an office, testimony procedure or craft privilege. The disputed story of an educated scribe using a masculine name must not be converted into proof of a literal universal Pope or a settled account of the alleged procession birth.

        Dependence can arise through tenancy, debt, service, household membership or protection. These forms are not interchangeable. A promise made under hunger or threat may be enforceable in a powerful person's court without being freely chosen. The existence of a written contract does not settle whether its terms are just.

        Public records can expose abuse and also preserve it. Private records can protect vulnerable testimony and also conceal wrongdoing. Destroying an oppressive institution's archive may also destroy the evidence its victims need. Keeping records accurate and making them appropriately accessible are separate responsibilities.

        Claims of descent are political evidence, not automatic commands. A recovered coastal charter can restore household rights without restoring an extinct kingdom. Pulling a sword from a stone can create public expectation without establishing a lawful succession.

    TECHNOLOGY, MAGIC AND THE MATERIAL WORLD:

        The present economy combines stone fortifications, domes, vaults, cisterns, mills, bridges, drainage works, hoists, specialist furnaces, advanced armor, crossbows, mature craft guilds and extensive written commerce. These abilities developed unevenly and moved between regions through apprenticeship, migration, trade and war.

        Severia's sophisticated administration survived because its metropolitan core endured. Azharad's scholarship predates Amyd because the reformer inherited an older civilization. Loxford can import good tools while retaining restrictive social customs. Technical sophistication and political liberty do not rise in a single sequence.

        Paper and parchment circulate alongside specialist copying, legal formularies, account books and translated manuscripts. Literacy varies by occupation and household. Access to a competent clerk creates power. Universal cheap books and mass printing are not established features of the setting.

        Banks, deposits, credit, sureties and shared commercial risk support trade. Their operation depends upon trust and enforceable agreements. The Great Market's instantaneous transactions remain a gameplay service; they do not prove that all goods throughout history moved without carriers, roads or ships.

        Coastal pilots use practical star knowledge, charts and instruments. Historical shipping does not add a required sailing, flying or deep-diving mechanic. No gunpowder weapon system is introduced by the setting's late-medieval comparison. Existing fantasy material tiers and equipment definitions take precedence over real-world metallurgy.

        Magic is powerful and materially consequential. It can affect combat, perception, movement, fire, identity and the world through the established systems. Its existence does not imply unlimited food, labor, transport, resurrection or protection. Skill, access, preparation and actual supported effects matter. A magical achievement can be rare without every common laborer being foolish for using ordinary tools.

        The three spellbooks remain Sun, Moon and Fog. Religious history adds no fourth school, hidden deity, new monster family or automatic moral alignment. Faith protection remains subject to the game's established rules. No historical relic overrides those rules merely because a song praises it.

    HISTORICAL LAYERS IN THE LANDSCAPE:

        New buildings often rest on older foundations. Reliefs are reused as lintels, obsolete measures survive in market weights, an imperial road serves people who resent imperial rule, and old graves constrain a lord who would otherwise prefer a new wall.

        Ruins have different causes. Some were burned in war; others lost their water, population, patrons or repair crews. Empty Summer houses were sometimes dismantled to rebuild a survivor's home. A collapsed roof does not always conceal a royal crime or supernatural catastrophe.

        The common history is visible in ordinary evidence: a winter wage tally paid by several communities; a sacred image repaired after another Sun authority condemned it; an injury award followed by an unpaid receipt; an imported spice in a household recipe; a widow's successful petition under an unpopular ruler; a guest cup preserved because Amyd once used it.

        Workers preserve knowledge scholars may lack. A repairer knows why the bridge leaks. A miner knows which inscription was recut. A cook knows which alleged famine occurred during a season of abundant fish. A household member can recognize an omitted name even when the official list is elegantly copied.

        Songs, feasts, recipes, clothing and borrowed words preserve history alongside monuments and chronicles. Pleasure, loyalty, faith and affection are genuine. Betrayal matters because there are worthwhile lives and relationships to damage.

    LEGENDS, RELICS AND CAMPAIGN CONTINUITY:

        BRANTOR, CORIN AND GOGMAR:
            Their landing tradition belongs around 2400 BCE. Material evidence supports early arrivals and settlement but does not establish every heroic episode, a separate ancient giant species, uninterrupted imperial descent or a present right to sovereignty.

        THE THREEFOLD VESSEL AND THE LANTERN:
            The reconstructable Compact dates to 730 BCE, substantial Lantern works to 690 BCE, the major Sun-era redaction to 96 CE and a partial renewal to 469 CE. These layers explain differences in vocabulary and custody. Three obligations never establish three gods. The Lantern is a signal station sustained by work, not a device capable of abolishing Fog.

        GANDORIAN AND THE FORGE SWORD:
            The sword belongs to a tradition of service and stewardship rather than an automatic succession test. Gandorian is the surviving heir of a coastal household absorbed generations earlier. Godric's attempt to have him drowned and the murder of his sisters occurred within Gandorian's own lifetime, not during the ancient absorption. Yannis's protection, the surviving charter and the political handling of evidence remain central. Gandorian is distinct from Arthur, Horen and the player. His rights do not automatically confer Loxford or Severia's crown.

        HOREN'S HOUSEHOLD:
            Horen's maritime inheritance is separate from Gandorian's coastal claim. Similar seals, institutions or historical pressures do not make the families one secret royal line. Household recovery and the safety of living people remain their own concerns.

        SUNDAR, SUMAK AND MALINOR:
            Their story comes from a distant third-century realm. Its arrival through translated copies explains its importance at Azharad's court. Sumak's supposed sorcery and the details of Sundar's victory remain subject to contradictory accounts. The final page prevents the victor's wrongdoing from disappearing behind his liberation story.

        GERBERT'S HEAD:
            The oldest attributed components belong to approximately 300-330 CE. Later repairs and log entries matter. Its answers can be incomplete, symbolic or mistaken. Comparisons with the Silver Branch, dispatch copies, Valgard orders and Hojimo receipts establish limited findings; the device cannot prophesy Artemios's guilt or identify the original schedule order-giver with certainty.

        ISKANDAR'S GATE:
            The fortified defile belongs to the conqueror's military tradition of 1120-1090 BCE and later maintenance. It channels danger, protects a bottleneck and supports signals. The gate does not imprison a new human nation, an additional monster species or the Fog itself.

        SIGFRID AND THE NIBLUNG HOARD:
            Their household conflict belongs approximately to 220-180 BCE. Accounts disagree over motive and exact events. The blood bath does not confer proven invulnerability. The recovered anatomical evidence can inform observation without guaranteeing that every Dragon has an identical weakness.

        ERVETH, THE LAST WARDEN:
            Erveth ruled his eastern holding from 472 to 440 BCE and died in his final Ashmaw expedition. His earlier success depended upon people and a functioning support network. His remains are not a prophecy, a player ancestor or evidence that Aurel secretly died in his place. Living Beorren is a different person.

        AUREL:
            His reign ended in 1249 BCE even though his preserved sleep continues. Awakening him supplies testimony and personal choices, not a new present sovereignty. He may seek recognition for the names of his dead without claiming their former lands from current inhabitants.

        OGIER AND MORGEN:
            Ogier's dates precede the oldest surviving Loxford register by roughly two centuries. His perceived brief absence and the records remain in tension. Morgen's hospitality lamp is a specific preserved arrangement, not a universal mechanism for immortality. Ogier can resume ordinary life and can be injured or killed.

        BRANN AND THE SILVER BRANCH:
            The westward voyage, a year among the kept and a return centuries later are preserved in conflicting measurements. Tide records, wear and family generations cannot conclusively distinguish altered time, allegory and copying errors. Publication must preserve this uncertainty rather than assign an invented definitive voyage date.

        ORFEON AND THE HARP:
            The hidden court preserves echoes and memory through Fog. Recognition between Orfeon and his wife can complete their remembered story without resurrecting them as living people. The encounter establishes neither a new fae species nor a third deity.

        THE CHILDREN OF LIR:
            Their name-stones preserve family history, Moon-kept memory and Fog echoes. The bell reveals a kept fate rather than reversing centuries. Bird imagery in the legend does not add a new animal species or make every metaphor a literal transformation record.

        DEIRA, NAOS AND ALBA:
            Alba is an old name for the western highlands. Their exile and the false pardon belong to an older political betrayal whose exact chronology remains disputed. Recovering graves and letters does not create another present kingdom or prove the prophecy said to have attended Deira's birth.

        ROLAN, OLIVAR AND GANEL:
            Their rearguard disaster belongs to an earlier Severian campaign whose exact year remains unsettled. Rolan delayed the horn through pride, and an order was altered before reaching him. Ganel's knowing betrayal is not proved merely by the altered copy. Later copying under an office associated with Artemios must be distinguished from the original campaign; his later custody does not make him responsible for an event before his lifetime.

        OTHER HISTORICAL TALES:
            The Fian are a historical wilderness fellowship, not a player class. The exiled Cid's posthumous charge remains a later claim rather than a confirmed general method of animating the dead. Godeva's reduced levy is better supported than every detail of the ride; the voyeur tale is a later addition. Tellen's escape route has evidence, but the later ambush and bailiff's identity remain less certain. The priest-king's letter is forged or heavily embellished and establishes no verified supernatural ruler in another playable realm. Other local tales may remain without exact dates where their evidence cannot supply them.

        CURRENT AND FUTURE EVENTS:
            Gowren's penitence, Elyra's recovery and wishes, the protection of Emren, the fate of Veyla and Teren, household inheritances, Merlin's sanctuary and testimony, and the player's handling of evidence remain governed by their authored quests.

            Rostamir and Sohran's confrontation, the exposure of Guinevere and Lancelot's affair, the fracture of the Round Table, Igor's expedition and the final Ashmaw confrontation take place after the opening date. They are not already completed historical events. Fixed tragedies remain fixed where authored, while variable outcomes are not preselected by this history.

            The player is not a secret heir, chosen vessel or prophesied ruler. No ancient genealogy, tally mark, sword, preserved king or final record changes that fact.

            The final political settlement can renew shared obligations with local safeguards, accept bounded imperial coordination with enforceable checks, or rely on more modest local lights. No outcome abolishes Fog, proves one faith inherently evil or turns a ruler into an unquestionable savior.

    THE INHERITANCE OF THE PRESENT:

        Richard defends the discretion holding his allies together. Phillip wants sworn law to survive personal favoritism. Fahad wants household authority and profitable roads. Torkell wants patrols to return. Dracula wants his valley protected and his private arrangements unexamined. Arthur wants refuge to mean something. Leonidas wants the empire's bargains to endure another succession. Artemios wants enough power to prevent the disaster he once watched, and increasingly manufactures dependence to obtain it.

        Their motives can be understood without declaring their conduct equally just. They possess different information, obligations, fears and opportunities to act. Some will lie, some will sacrifice, and some will discover that the virtue for which they are praised has become an excuse for harming somebody else.

        Beneath them, the world continues because people fish, quarry, harvest, mend, carry, teach, cook, bury, remember and repair. The history of Ashenwrath belongs to those people as well as its crowns.
